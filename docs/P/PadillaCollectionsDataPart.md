# Position + moment

Padilla, Scates Kettler, and Shorish write from the mature phase of the Collections as Data movement after two Mellon-supported programmes and a 2023 international summit. The report reflects on twelve institutional cohort projects and wider international participation, shifting the field from an initial emphasis on making cultural-heritage collections computationally tractable toward sustainability, community governance, organizational capacity, AI, sovereignty, documentation and harm mitigation. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 3–5, 8–16]

# The author’s main move

The report’s main move is to recast collections-as-data as a sociotechnical and institutional practice rather than a file-conversion exercise. Computational tractability is only one part of the work. The authors place decisions about whether material should become data, who participates, how decisions are documented, which organizational structures sustain the work, and how harms from extraction, bias, AI and unequal infrastructures are mitigated inside the definition of responsible computational use. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 3–5, 8, 10–16, 18]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Collections-as-data is most useful as a flexible coordinating concept rather than a fixed technical model. **Author claim.** Padilla, Scates Kettler, and Shorish describe collections-as-data as a “boundary object” that can remain recognizable across different professional, disciplinary and institutional settings while being adapted to local needs. **Evidence.** The introduction explicitly frames collections-as-data through Star and Griesemer’s boundary-object concept and then restates it as simultaneously weakly and strongly structured, abstract and concrete, locally situated and broadly framed. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 3–4] **Evidence-supported claim.** The report treats collections-as-data as a shared practice framework capable of supporting coordination across heterogeneous contexts rather than as a universal data model. **Researcher inference.** The DDR computational research instrument should likewise be described as a situated implementation of principles for archival activation, not as a general template for all archives. **Warrant.** The report locates value in adaptability across contexts while retaining a common identity around responsible computational use. **Boundary.** Boundary-object flexibility does not mean that every local implementation is equally responsible or technically sound. **Consequence.** The thesis can make a transferable methodological proposition without claiming that its corpus structure, interface or ML stack should be copied wholesale elsewhere. **Practice cross-check.** Frame the DDR system as one bounded research instrument whose provenance-aware practices may transfer while its specific corpus, UMAP layout and retrieval configuration remain local to DDR.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Computational availability is not an inevitable or universally desirable end state for cultural-heritage collections. **Author claim.** The authors state that not all collections have or should have an inevitable expression as data and that some should not be openly accessible where openness could cause harm or violate cultural expectations. **Evidence.** The introduction gives examples including social-protest collections under authoritarian regimes and culturally inappropriate universal openness, while also linking collections-as-data work to histories of knowledge extraction from minoritized communities. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 3–4] **Evidence-supported claim.** Decisions to datafy, expose or computationally process collections are ethical and political choices rather than automatic consequences of digitisation. **Researcher inference.** The DDR corpus boundary should be treated as a designed evidential boundary whose inclusions, exclusions, rights conditions and access decisions must be explicit. **Warrant.** If computational tractability can create harm or reproduce extraction, then making material machine-actionable is itself an intervention requiring justification. **Boundary.** The DDR archive does not automatically present the same sovereignty risks as Indigenous, protest or community-held collections discussed in the report. **Consequence.** The thesis should avoid equating “more data” or “more open” with better archival activation. **Practice cross-check.** Keep rights status, corpus inclusion rules and source provenance inspectable, and preserve the distinction between material available to the research corpus and material that may exist outside it.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Community participation is part of the epistemic quality of collections-as-data, not merely an outreach activity. **Author claim.** The cohort theme “No collections-as-data about us without us” states that communities represented in collections should be engaged in creation and use through mechanisms such as advisory boards, team composition and calls for input. **Evidence.** The report links community involvement both to respect and trust and to more generative research and educational possibilities. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 8] **Evidence-supported claim.** Representation and participation influence how collections are structured, interpreted and made usable, so participation can change the resulting knowledge infrastructure. **Researcher inference.** For DDR, oral histories and stakeholder perspectives should not be treated as decorative validation around a finished computational system; they can challenge description, attribution, terminology and the interpretation of machine-surfaced relations. **Warrant.** If represented communities bring knowledge that changes the structure and use of collection data, their involvement has epistemic consequences. **Boundary.** The report does not prescribe one universal participatory method or imply that every historical collection has an identifiable contemporary community with authority over interpretation. **Consequence.** The thesis should position testimony and stakeholder engagement as part of interpretative cross-reading, while retaining clear distinctions between archival record, recollection and researcher synthesis. **Practice cross-check.** Use DDR oral-history testimony to test and complicate archival descriptions and computational associations rather than to overwrite documentary evidence.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Documentation is a core part of computational provenance because future users need to understand why a collection was built as it was. **Author claim.** The authors identify “Good documentation is crucial” as a cohort theme and argue that decisions and rationales must be documented across creation, maintenance and use. **Evidence.** They state that documentation should explain motivation, scope and completeness and that future stewards need to know why the collection was constructed in a particular way in order to maintain its intent and purpose. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 8] **Evidence-supported claim.** Reproducibility and responsible reuse depend not only on files and code but on documented decisions about scope, transformation and stewardship. **Researcher inference.** The DDR version history, corpus receipts, source-to-chunk provenance, model versions, interface states and design-decision records are not administrative overhead; they are part of the research evidence. **Warrant.** Without those records, later users cannot determine how computational outputs relate to collection construction. **Boundary.** Documentation cannot remove every ambiguity or make a complex pipeline fully reproducible across future software environments. **Consequence.** The examined research state should include enough documentary evidence to reconstruct the principal decisions that shaped the corpus and instrument. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve release receipts, named snapshots, data-model documentation, retrieval/failure logs and system-state records as part of the submitted practice evidence.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Sustainable computational work depends more on embedded practices and principles than on the transfer of supposedly universal institutional models. **Author claim.** Summit participants found many cohort projects closer to proof-of-concepts than transferable models, while projects integrated into established workflows or position scopes appeared more sustainable. **Evidence.** The model-assessment section concludes that sharing practices and considering principles may be more effective for sustaining collections-as-data work than promoting a singular “model” approach. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 10] **Evidence-supported claim.** Institutional fit and integration matter more than formal similarity to an abstract model. **Researcher inference.** The doctoral contribution should therefore be framed as a provenance-aware practice and methodological proposition rather than as a universal archival-computational framework. **Warrant.** The report’s own multi-institutional experimentation found transferability limited where projects remained special initiatives rather than embedded practices. **Boundary.** A PhD research instrument has different sustainability requirements from a permanent memory-organization service. **Consequence.** The thesis should distinguish clearly between research readiness and claims of institutional deployment or general usability. **Practice cross-check.** Keep the DDR instrument’s evaluation bounded to doctoral research use, while documenting which practices—provenance, versioning, scoped missingness, claim-evidence checking—could plausibly transfer elsewhere.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Critical computational practice must account for uneven cultural, linguistic, legal and infrastructural conditions because these shape what becomes machine-readable and visible. **Author claim.** The Vancouver Summit observations identify cultural bias, gatekeeping, sovereignty, different AI and data regulations, variable digitisation, dominance of English in OCR and AI, uneven data fluency and financial asymmetries as international challenges. **Evidence.** The report also identifies opportunities in shared infrastructure, multilingual resources, FAIR/CARE adoption, distributed governance and linking projects across locales. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 10–12] **Evidence-supported claim.** Computational access is conditioned by infrastructures and institutional resources that are distributed unevenly and can privilege particular languages, regions and forms of description. **Researcher inference.** DDR machine legibility should be analysed partly as an effect of digitisation quality, cataloguing practice, OCR, metadata density and model behaviour, not simply as a property of the historical record. **Warrant.** If technical and linguistic conditions vary, then what computation can retrieve or connect varies with them. **Boundary.** The report’s international observations are broad qualitative themes rather than a controlled comparative study. **Consequence.** The thesis should keep descriptive absence, digitisation/OCR failure, corpus absence and retrieval failure analytically distinct. **Practice cross-check.** Record OCR defects, sparse metadata, excluded non-PID text and retrieval failures as separate states when evaluating DDR missingness and legibility.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** Responsible computational work requires a deliberate trade-off between analytical potential and the harms introduced by speed, scale, automation and AI reuse. **Author claim.** The report characterises the field’s orientation as sustaining computational potential while mitigating harms such as amplified bias, and it explicitly contrasts this with the emphasis on speed often associated with computational work. **Evidence.** The authors describe a “deliberative, historically conscious pace” and later note both the benefits of AI for OCR, metadata and scaled research services and the risks of collections being used as training data without memory-organization consent. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 14–15] **Evidence-supported claim.** AI can expand access and capacity, but responsible use requires governance over consent, mission, bias, policy and the conditions under which data are produced in the first place. **Researcher inference.** The DDR instrument should value bounded, inspectable and slower interpretation over maximal generation speed or answer coverage where those goals conflict. **Warrant.** The report places responsibility inside system design and organizational decision-making rather than after the fact. **Boundary.** The report does not supply technical evaluation criteria for RAG, embeddings or LLM inference quality. **Consequence.** The thesis needs complementary operational literature for retrieval adequacy and model evaluation, while using Padilla et al. to set the institutional and ethical frame around those techniques. **Practice cross-check.** Maintain fail-closed retrieval behaviour, explicit evidential limits, source-linked answers and scoped missingness rather than forcing synthesis where the corpus cannot support it.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Collections-as-data →** the concept, community and area of practice concerned with the responsible development and computational use of collections as data. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 18]
- **Datafication →** making material machine-actionable and amenable to computational analysis; distinct from digitisation because a digital surrogate is not necessarily structured or described for computation. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 18]
- **Data sovereignty →** the agency of individuals or groups over the collection, storage, interpretation and sharing of their data. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, p. 18]
- **Sustainable human infrastructure →** an adaptive organizational support system that can absorb staff transition while sustaining documentation, technical expertise and organizational practice. [@PadillaCollectionsDataPart, pp. 15–16]

# My response

This source moves the critical computational lens beyond an argument about algorithms alone. The important unit is the whole sociotechnical arrangement: the source collection, the decision to datafy it, the people represented, the institutional structures that sustain it, the documentation that explains it, and the computational systems that act on it. For DDR, that means the archive does not become analytically neutral once it has been converted into chunks, embeddings or interface objects. The conditions of datafication remain part of the evidential story.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** Computational activation begins before retrieval: the decision to make archival material machine-actionable, the documentation of that transformation, and the governance of its reuse already shape what the archive can become legible as.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Padilla, Scates Kettler, and Shorish extend the computational cluster outward from algorithms to the institutional and social conditions that make computational work possible. Manning supplies the operational vocabulary for retrieval, relevance, similarity and evaluation; Drucker shows that computational representations and interfaces are epistemic constructions rather than transparent views; Karp demonstrates how computational similarity can create new archival relational views while leaving interpretation with the archivist. Padilla et al. add the prior and surrounding layer: the archive must first be datafied, governed, documented and sustained, and that process carries obligations to communities, rights, sovereignty and institutional mission. For the DDR thesis, the combined proposition is that computational activation is a chain of constructed conditions—from collection and datafication through retrieval, representation and interface—each of which can create new legibility while also introducing new exclusions or authority effects. The empirical task is therefore to show, through the DDR instrument, that these conditions remain inspectable enough for computationally surfaced relations to be treated as research propositions rather than historical facts.
