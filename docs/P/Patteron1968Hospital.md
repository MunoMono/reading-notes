# The author’s main move (1 sentence)
They try to show that hospital design problems are really interface problems by tracing how equipment, buildings, staff practice, and patient needs interact in order to argue for earlier, continuous collaboration between designers rather than sectional solutions.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Patterson shows that DDR practice depended on early collaboration and detailed user-study, and that late consultation produced inferior design outcomes.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** In the children’s ward example, the architects had already fixed key decisions before the equipment designer was brought in, forcing him to work around advanced building plans and ceiling fixtures rather than shaping the whole solution from the start. Patterson says that had the designer been consulted earlier, “a more satisfactory solution could have been found with no impairment of standards.” She also notes her own responsibility within the team for information collection and analysis, and for detailed study of user needs. `[@Hospital, pp. 905–907]`

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Patterson treats design change as a negotiation between artefact, practice, and institutional habit, not as a one-way imposition of better objects.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** She shows that new equipment may require changed practice, but practice also constrains design. Nurses failed to notice height-adjustable beds already installed; emergency bed tilt had to “catch” the nurse before she ran for blocks or an elevator; medicine trolley procedures could be encouraged but not enforced by elegant design; and bedmaking tests showed how small changes in form created new bodily problems. She concludes that each point of contact between user and equipment can require adjustment from one or the other, and that continuous dialogue between architect and equipment designer is necessary because they are “members of the same team.” `[@Hospital, pp. 907–908]`
- **Warrant (my words):** This matters because it makes design research look less like top-down problem solving and more like the iterative management of mutual adaptation across users, routines, and environments.
- **So what for my thesis:** Patterson provides a valuable historical source for showing that DDR knowledge was already attentive to situated practice, institutional habit, and the limits of design prescription.

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)
- **Interface:** the point of contact where user, equipment, and building meet and where the real design problem often appears; I will use this as a key analytic term for locating contested design knowledge in the archive. `[@Hospital, p. 905]`
- **Sectional solutions:** solutions produced by demarcated specialist responsibility rather than continuous interdisciplinary dialogue; I will use this to describe organisational failure in design research settings. `[@Hospital, p. 908]`
- **User needs:** needs studied in detail through observation and analysis rather than assumed abstractly; I will use this cautiously as a historical DDR term, since Patterson shows these needs are negotiated within wider institutional constraints. `[@Hospital, pp. 905–908]`
- **Value for money:** a measure that includes negative as well as positive results, such as avoiding injury to patients and staff; useful for showing that evaluation exceeded upfront capital cost. `[@Hospital, p. 908]`
