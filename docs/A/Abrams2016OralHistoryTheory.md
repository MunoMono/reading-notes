# Thesis job

**How this source moves the primary research question forward:** Abrams gives the thesis a rigorous way to treat oral-history interviews as testamentary traces that are simultaneously evidence about the past and situated, co-produced narratives whose meaning depends on memory, subjectivity, performance, power and interpretation.

**How this source bears on the secondary question:** Revisiting DDR-period ideas through living witnesses requires distinguishing retrospective testimony from contemporary records while taking seriously what memory, later interpretation and present identity reveal about the continuing meaning of those ideas.

**Why I’m reading this now:** The oral-history programme is one of the thesis’s principal evidence routes, and Abrams supplies the operational theory needed to prevent testimony from being treated either as simple fact retrieval or as unreliable anecdote.

**Where it sits in my argument:** Operational literature because it provides the interpretative rules and cautions through which testimony can be produced, analysed and cross-read with archival records.

# Position + moment

Abrams’s second edition presents oral history as a mature interdisciplinary field whose theory has developed from practice. The book treats memory, self, subjectivity, intersubjectivity, narrative, performance, power, trauma and ethics as interconnected dimensions of how oral-history evidence is produced and interpreted. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. ix–xi, 1–17]

# The author’s main move

Abrams rejects the idea that oral history is simply a technique for extracting facts from witnesses. The interview is both a research act and a produced record: a present-tense event of communication in which narrator and interviewer co-create a memory narrative that carries factual information, subjective meaning, performance, cultural framing and power relations. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 1–7, 18–27, 54–76]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Oral history is both a method of inquiry and a record produced by that inquiry. **Author claim.** Abrams defines oral history as both the process of conducting and recording interviews about the past and the narrative account that results. **Evidence.** In the introduction she states that practice and theory—doing and interpreting—are entwined and that the interview must be understood not only for what is said but how, why and with what meaning it is said. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 1–3] **Evidence-supported claim.** Oral testimony cannot be separated cleanly from the conditions of its production. **Researcher inference.** DDR interviews should be archived and analysed as created research records with their own provenance rather than treated as transparent access to an earlier period. **Warrant.** The interviewer’s questions and the interview event shape the resulting trace. **Boundary.** This does not make oral testimony arbitrary; it changes the kind of evidence it provides. **Consequence.** The thesis should record interview date, participants, context and question route alongside transcript excerpts. **Practice cross-check.** Link each oral-history passage used in synthesis to its interview identity and distinguish it from contemporary DDR documents.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** The value of oral history exceeds factual recovery because testimony communicates interpretation and meaning. **Author claim.** Abrams charts the field’s movement from “recovery history,” in which oral evidence was often cross-checked against documents to establish facts, toward analysis of memory narratives as cultural productions. **Evidence.** Her account of the 1970s–80s turn stresses that oral sources contain factual statements but also memory, ideology, desire and present-day interpretation; she describes oral history as accessing signification as well as information. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 4–7] **Evidence-supported claim.** A discrepancy between testimony and documentary evidence may itself be historically meaningful rather than simply a failed recollection. **Researcher inference.** DDR retrospective testimony can show how design-research ideas were later understood, valued or contested even where it does not establish a contemporary event. **Warrant.** Remembering is itself evidence of later meaning-making. **Boundary.** Retrospective meaning cannot be substituted for contemporary evidence of action or intent. **Consequence.** The thesis should preserve the distinction between remembered interpretation and documentary chronology. **Practice cross-check.** In testimony-archive cross-reading, label later recollection separately from contemporary record and compare rather than merge them.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Subjectivity is an evidential feature of oral history, not a defect to be eliminated. **Author claim.** Abrams argues that oral historians deliberately seek personal experience, emotion, political views and reflective meaning, and that oral sources show not only what people did but what they believed and now think they did. **Evidence.** The discussion of subjectivity identifies the reflective self as a defining property of oral testimony and contrasts this with a narrow search for facts. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 21–22] **Evidence-supported claim.** Testimony provides access to historically situated and retrospectively interpreted experience. **Researcher inference.** Interviewees’ accounts of DDR can legitimately be used to analyse perceived disciplinary identity, values and conflict, provided those perceptions are not converted into unqualified statements of past fact. **Warrant.** The evidence concerns meaning as experienced and narrated by a person. **Boundary.** Subjectivity does not establish representativeness across the whole department. **Consequence.** Oral-history claims should specify whether they concern event, perception, memory or later interpretation. **Practice cross-check.** Use interview excerpts to illuminate contested understandings while checking factual chronology against archive records.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Memory is active, mutable and socially mediated, so change in recollection is analytically significant. **Author claim.** Abrams treats memory’s imperfections, mutability and susceptibility to outside influence as central to oral-history interpretation rather than reasons to dismiss it. **Evidence.** She describes memory as an active process produced in the interview through questions, cultural narratives, prompts and the respondent’s present circumstances; repeated tellings can change. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 22–23, 78–105] **Evidence-supported claim.** Oral testimony is a present reconstruction of the past, not a fixed recording retrieved intact from memory. **Researcher inference.** Differences between DDR interview accounts and archival traces should be analysed for what they reveal about later disciplinary memory as well as checked for factual accuracy. **Warrant.** The gap between event and recollection is part of the source’s historical meaning. **Boundary.** Not every discrepancy carries meaningful cultural significance; some may simply be error or uncertainty. **Consequence.** The thesis should not force testimony into documentary agreement. **Practice cross-check.** Record contradictions and uncertainty explicitly in evidence packets rather than resolving them through generated synthesis.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Intersubjectivity means the interviewer participates in producing the testimony. **Author claim.** Abrams defines intersubjectivity as the relationship between interviewer and narrator through which a shared narrative is created; a different interviewer can elicit a different version. **Evidence.** Chapter 4 states that both subjectivities are present and that there can be no pretence that the interviewer is a neutral presence. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 54–56] **Evidence-supported claim.** The questions, identity, gestures and assumptions of the researcher are part of the evidence-producing context. **Researcher inference.** My position as a designer and DDR researcher must be treated as part of how the oral histories are elicited and interpreted. **Warrant.** Interview data is relationally generated. **Boundary.** Intersubjectivity does not imply that every statement is caused by the interviewer. **Consequence.** Reflexive documentation of interview design and researcher position is methodologically necessary. **Practice cross-check.** Retain discussion guides and note where prompts, follow-up questions or prior archival knowledge shaped a passage.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Orality and performance carry meaning that can be reduced when testimony is converted to transcript or clipped into searchable fragments. **Author claim.** Abrams argues that meaning lies not only in content but also in delivery, gesture, tone, narrative form and performance; her second-edition preface also notes that digital coding, clipping and indexing change how memory stories are heard and interpreted. **Evidence.** The book treats performance as inseparable from content and warns that working through digital files, clips and transcripts has interpretative consequences. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. ix–x, 20–21, 130–152] **Evidence-supported claim.** Textualised testimony is a derived representation of a richer recorded event. **Researcher inference.** Embedding or retrieving transcript chunks from DDR interviews risks stripping away sequence, hesitation, tone and narrative context. **Warrant.** Computational segmentation changes the representational form through which testimony is interpreted. **Boundary.** Text transcripts remain necessary and useful analytical tools. **Consequence.** Important testimony claims should be checked against larger transcript context and, where necessary, audio. **Practice cross-check.** Keep interview-level identity and surrounding passages when testimony enters retrieval or synthesis.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** “Shared authority” does not remove the structural power imbalance between researcher and narrator. **Author claim.** Abrams shows that the interviewer helps confer authority to speak, while researchers retain substantial control over transcription, interpretation and publication; collaboration can redistribute authority but has practical and ethical limits. **Evidence.** Her power chapter traces how researcher authority increases as material moves from interview to interpretation and publication and warns against assuming that respondents simply “speak for themselves.” [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 153–168] **Evidence-supported claim.** Oral-history participation can be collaborative without becoming power-neutral. **Researcher inference.** DDR interviewees’ words should not be presented as raw voices untouched by selection, editing and thesis argument. **Warrant.** The researcher determines what is excerpted, related and published. **Boundary.** The degree of authority can vary with project design and participant involvement. **Consequence.** The thesis should make quotation, interpretation and editorial decisions visible where they materially affect meaning. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve full transcript provenance behind selected excerpts and distinguish participant statement from my analytical framing.

## Claim 8

**Claim.** Ethical oral history requires attention to harms created by the interview itself, not only formal consent. **Author claim.** Abrams cautions that speaking is not always therapeutic and that interviews can trigger unresolved memories or expose participants to emotional cost; ethical review must consider vulnerability and the consequences of elicitation. **Evidence.** The trauma-and-ethics chapter emphasises informed consent and confidentiality but warns against assuming that creating a space to speak is inherently beneficial. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 175–192] **Evidence-supported claim.** Researcher responsibility extends through the consequences of eliciting, recording, storing and publishing testimony. **Researcher inference.** DDR interviews with older former staff or students require sensitive handling even when the project is not framed as trauma research. **Warrant.** An interview can surface difficult memories unexpectedly. **Boundary.** Abrams’s strongest examples concern crisis and trauma oral history, which is not the primary focus of the DDR study. **Consequence.** Ethical interviewing should include participant control over sensitive material and researcher awareness of unanticipated disclosure. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (cross-check interview consent, withdrawal, transcript review and handling of sensitive passages against the oral-history protocol).

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Oral history →** both the practice of eliciting and recording testimony about the past and the produced narrative record that results. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 1–2]
- **Intersubjectivity →** the relational process through which interviewer and narrator jointly shape the memory narrative produced in an interview. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 54–56]
- **Shared authority →** an attempt to redistribute interpretative and project authority between researcher and participants without assuming that structural inequalities disappear. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 153–168]
- **Memory narrative →** a present act of reconstructing past experience through personal memory, cultural discourse and the interview context. [@Abrams2016OralHistoryTheory, pp. 22–23, 78–105]

# My response

Abrams gives me the rule I need for DDR testimony: neither downgrade it because memory is imperfect nor promote it to the status of a contemporary record. Oral history is valuable because it combines evidence about past experience with evidence about how that past is remembered and made meaningful now. The thesis becomes stronger when those two evidential functions remain visible.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** DDR oral histories are not transparent substitutes for missing documents but co-produced memory records whose evidential value lies in both what they recall and how the past is reconstructed from the standpoint of the present.

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** Abrams provides a broad theory of oral history; not every interpretative model in the book will be necessary for the DDR interviews.

**Risk:** Treating subjectivity as automatically meaningful could become as reductive as treating memory as automatically unreliable. The thesis must still assess what each passage can and cannot establish.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Abrams and Harris converge on a crucial point from different directions: neither documentary archives nor oral testimony provide an unmediated past. Harris calls the archival record a constructed sliver shaped by creators, archivists and users; Abrams shows that testimony is actively produced through memory, interviewer interaction and present interpretation. ISAD(G) then supplies the formal provenance and contextual structures through which documentary traces are described. Together these sources justify the thesis’s evidential separation of contemporary record, later testimony and researcher synthesis. What remains to be demonstrated is how individual DDR cases change when these trace types are placed beside one another without forcing agreement.
