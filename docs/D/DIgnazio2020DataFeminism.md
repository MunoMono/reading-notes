# Position + moment

Published in 2020, *Data Feminism* brings intersectional feminist thought into data science and data communication. The book is structured around seven principles—examine power, challenge power, elevate emotion and embodiment, rethink binaries and hierarchies, embrace pluralism, consider context, and make labour visible—and explicitly connects these to situated knowledge, intersectionality and the politics of classification. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 17–19]

# The author’s main move

D’Ignazio and Klein argue that data practices are never detached from power. Data are produced through social relations, classification systems, institutional priorities and human labour. Their intervention is practical as much as theoretical: data work should expose power, preserve context, value multiple forms of knowledge, question categories and make otherwise invisible labour visible. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 8–19]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Data work should begin by examining unequal power rather than assuming technical neutrality. **Author claim.** D’Ignazio and Klein define data feminism as a way of thinking about the uses and limits of data that begins from the fact that power is unequally distributed and asks how data practices reinforce or challenge that distribution. **Evidence.** In the introduction they state that standard data-science practices can reproduce existing inequalities and that data feminism should first identify those processes and then use data to challenge them. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 8–9] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source treats technical practice as inseparable from institutional and social power. **Researcher inference.** DDR computational visibility should therefore be tested against existing archival hierarchies rather than treated as an automatically corrective layer. **Warrant.** A retrieval system trained on uneven records may reproduce the archive’s existing visibility structure. **Boundary.** The book does not show that every computational ordering reproduces social domination. **Consequence.** The thesis should ask who becomes more or less legible after datafication, retrieval and visualisation. **Practice cross-check.** Compare retrieval frequency and semantic prominence for heavily documented senior figures against weakly attributed or sparsely catalogued contributors.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Classification systems are active interventions that can encode and reproduce structural inequality. **Author claim.** The authors’ principle “rethink binaries and hierarchies” treats acts of counting and classification as political decisions. **Evidence.** Their discussion of gender classification argues that seemingly simple binary categories can create harm and that analysts must ask whose interests are served by being counted and who may be harmed by the categories used. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 106–112] **Evidence-supported claim.** Categories are not neutral containers; they shape which distinctions become analytically visible. **Researcher inference.** DDR metadata fields, role categories, project labels and machine-generated clusters should be treated as interpretative structures rather than natural historical kinds. **Warrant.** Categorisation controls what can be grouped, compared, filtered and counted. **Boundary.** Some categories remain necessary for retrieval and analysis. **Consequence.** The thesis should document when categories are archival, researcher-defined or machine-derived. **Practice cross-check.** Keep catalogue metadata separate from derived facets, generated labels and semantic groupings.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Missing or weakly represented data can itself be evidence about institutional priorities, but absence must be interpreted cautiously. **Author claim.** D’Ignazio and Klein repeatedly show that what is counted reflects what institutions value and that uncounted experiences can remain structurally invisible. **Evidence.** In the maternal-health example they note that the absence of systematic counting was itself consequential, while their broader principle that “what gets counted counts” links measurement to institutional value. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 23–24, 97–112] **Evidence-supported claim.** Data absence can result from social and institutional decisions rather than from the non-existence of the underlying phenomenon. **Researcher inference.** Sparse DDR traces should prompt questions about documentary practice and attribution, not immediate conclusions that activity or participation did not occur. **Warrant.** Under-recording and selective recording can create patterned invisibility. **Boundary.** Missing data do not by themselves establish deliberate exclusion or prove the presence of an unrecorded actor. **Consequence.** The thesis should preserve scoped missingness and distinguish archival absence from historical non-existence. **Practice cross-check.** Treat “no retrieved evidence,” “no digitised evidence” and “no surviving archival evidence” as distinct claims.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Data cannot be interpreted responsibly without the context of their production. **Author claim.** The authors reject the idea that numbers “speak for themselves” and argue that social, political and historical context is necessary for accurate interpretation. **Evidence.** Chapter 6 shows how the same numerical results can support radically different interpretations depending on whether structural racism and the conditions of data collection are named; they explicitly reject raw-data thinking and describe data as already shaped by prior circumstances. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 159–167] **Evidence-supported claim.** Decontextualised numerical or visual outputs can misrepresent the phenomena they purport to describe. **Researcher inference.** Similarity scores, UMAP distances and retrieval rankings should never appear without source identity and archival context. **Warrant.** Computational outputs are transformations of historically situated source material. **Boundary.** Contextualisation does not require reproducing every aspect of a record’s history beside every output. **Consequence.** The interface and thesis must preserve enough provenance to move from computational representation back to archival source. **Practice cross-check.** Require evidence cards to link semantic outputs to PID-backed records, source passages and repository metadata.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Plural perspectives can improve knowledge production because no single standpoint is complete. **Author claim.** “Embrace pluralism” prioritises multiple perspectives, especially local, Indigenous and experiential forms of knowledge, rather than assuming a singular objective account. **Evidence.** The seven principles explicitly state that the most complete knowledge comes from synthesising multiple perspectives and that situated knowledge is central to the book’s feminist method. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 18–19] **Evidence-supported claim.** Robust interpretation benefits from juxtaposing different forms and locations of knowledge. **Researcher inference.** DDR oral testimony, archival records, catalogue metadata and computational representations should be read together without collapsing them into one evidential voice. **Warrant.** Each source type reveals and obscures different aspects of the research object. **Boundary.** Pluralism does not mean every account carries equal evidential weight for every historical proposition. **Consequence.** The thesis should preserve the distinction between source, testimony and synthesis while still allowing cross-reading. **Practice cross-check.** Use testimony-archive cross-reading to test, complicate and contextualise machine-surfaced relations.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Making labour visible is necessary for understanding how data products and knowledge infrastructures are produced. **Author claim.** The authors argue that data work is produced by many hands and that the labour behind visualisations, models and products is often hidden or undervalued. **Evidence.** Chapter 7 asks who collected, processed, designed and maintained data products, describes the “data supply chain,” and argues that “show your work” should trace outputs back to their material conditions and contributors. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 178–179, 200–201] **Evidence-supported claim.** Attribution is part of methodological transparency, not merely acknowledgment. **Researcher inference.** DDR analysis should examine both historical invisible labour inside the archive and present-day invisible labour inside the research instrument. **Warrant.** Both historical knowledge and computational outputs are collective productions whose apparent authorship can conceal contributors. **Boundary.** The source does not provide an archival attribution method for reconstructing undocumented labour. **Consequence.** The thesis should distinguish named authorship, recorded participation, inferred contribution and unverified possibility. **Practice cross-check.** Use project staff lists, acknowledgements, catalogue descriptions and oral histories to test differential attribution, while separately documenting the contemporary labour behind corpus preparation and system design.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** Feminist data practice is methodological when it changes process, not only when it changes subject matter. **Author claim.** D’Ignazio and Klein state that a project can be feminist in content, form or process, including through participatory and inclusive forms of knowledge production. **Evidence.** Their introduction explicitly differentiates feminist subject matter from feminist aesthetic and procedural choices. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 18–19] **Evidence-supported claim.** Feminist analysis can operate through how research is designed as well as through which topics it studies. **Researcher inference.** The DDR project’s feminist strand should therefore shape corpus decisions, interface logic, attribution rules and evidential language, not only a discrete case study about women in DDR. **Warrant.** If process itself distributes visibility and authority, then feminist critique belongs inside research design. **Boundary.** Calling a process participatory or inclusive does not automatically make it feminist or just. **Consequence.** The thesis should demonstrate where feminist principles materially altered the instrument or interpretative workflow. **Practice cross-check.** Record design changes that improve attribution visibility, preserve uncertainty, expose missingness or prevent machine-generated authority from overwriting archival evidence.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Data feminism →** a way of thinking about data, their uses and their limits, informed by direct experience, action and intersectional feminist thought. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 8–9]
- **Privilege hazard →** the difficulty people in structurally privileged positions can have in recognising oppression or harm outside their own lived experience. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 28–30]
- **Pluralism →** a commitment to multiple situated perspectives rather than a singular view-from-nowhere account. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 18–19]
- **Make labour visible →** trace data products back to the people, conditions and work that enabled them. [@DIgnazio2020DataFeminism, pp. 178–179, 200–201]
