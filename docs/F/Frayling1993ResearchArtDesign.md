# Position + moment (2–4 lines)
Frayling writes as Rector of the Royal College of Art in the first issue of *RCA Research Papers* in 1993/94, citing the College Charter and speaking into a policy environment where research funding, validation, and doctoral structures were reshaping higher education. The paper is overtly institutional as well as conceptual: it tries to clear up confusion around the “r word” in art, craft, and design and to provide practical categories for the College’s research culture. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 1, 5]`  
**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):** Frayling is often cited as if the into / through / for triad were a settled ontology. In this paper it is better read as a strategic institutional sorting device, developed to manage legitimacy, doctoral assessment, and resource questions in a post-polytechnic, post-DDR landscape. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 1, 4–5]`

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)
They try to stabilise what counts as research in art and design by unpacking stereotypes about artists, designers, and scientists and by proposing three categories of research in order to make research in the RCA context more legible, defensible, and practically assessable.

## Claim 1
- **Claim (plain):** Frayling argues that the debate about research in art and design has been distorted by stereotypes rather than clarified by practice.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** He opens by saying there are “almost as many definitions of research flying around in higher education right now, as there are reasons for promoting them,” and that much confusion has revolved around stereotypes of what research is, what it involves, and what it delivers. He then sets out three public images: the expressive artist, the style-obsessed or boffin designer, and the critical-rationalist scientist, and shows each to be inadequate. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 1–4]`
- **Warrant (my words):** This is the paper’s clearing operation. Frayling is not starting by defining a timeless essence of research; he is first dismantling the bad images that prevent art and design practices from being thought clearly in research terms.
- **So what for my thesis (a reusable sentence):** Frayling provides a post-DDR framework for showing that arguments about research in art and design were shaped as much by institutional and cultural stereotypes as by actual practice.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Frayling’s key distinction is that research in art and design falls into three different categories: into, through, and for.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** He ends by proposing “three categories (derived from Herbert Read)” that might suit what people in art and design actually do: “Research into art and design,” “Research through art and design,” and “Research for art and design.” He then defines the first as historical, aesthetic/perceptual, or theoretical research; the second as materials research, development work, or action research with documentation and contextualisation; and the third as research where the end product is an artefact and the thinking is embodied in it. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 4–5]`
- **Warrant (my words):** This is the paper’s most influential move because it shifts the debate from one blunt yes/no question about whether practice “counts” as research to a differentiated typology with different expectations of evidence and communication.
- **So what for my thesis:** Frayling gives me a strong way to describe how post-DDR RCA tried to classify relations between artefacts, documentation, and knowledge claims rather than treating all practice as either self-evidently research or self-evidently not research.
- **Practice cross-check:** This bears directly on your thesis method. Archive work is largely research **into** design; computational and visual activation could become research **through** design if documented; and any claim that an artefact alone “speaks for itself” should be handled carefully. ## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Frayling accepts research through practice, but he is sceptical about awarding research status where the artefact alone is taken as sufficient communication.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** For research through art and design he insists on a diary, report, or other means to “communicate the results,” which is what separates research from the gathering of reference materials. By contrast, in discussing research for art and design, he says the goal is not primarily communicable knowledge in verbal form but visual or imagistic communication, and notes that at the RCA they do not offer research degrees entirely for work where the art is said to “speak for itself”; the College tends to feel the goal there is “the art rather than the knowledge and understanding.” `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 5]`
- **Warrant (my words):** This is the practical gatekeeping core of the paper. Frayling is willing to widen the space of research, but only where a claim to knowledge can be articulated and assessed beyond the artefact itself.
- **So what for my thesis:** Frayling helps me show that post-DDR research culture at the RCA depended heavily on documentation, contextualisation, and communicable knowledge claims, not just on making.

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)
- **Research into art and design:** historical, perceptual, aesthetic, or theory-led inquiry about art and design; I will use this for archival and historiographic work that studies design from outside the artefact-producing act itself. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, p. 5]`
- **Research through art and design:** inquiry conducted by means of practice, where the practical activity is documented and contextualised so that results can be communicated; I will use this for studio or prototype work that generates analyzable knowledge. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, p. 5]`
- **Research for art and design:** gathering of reference material or embodied thinking directed primarily toward the artefact, where communicable knowledge may remain inside or subordinate to the work; I will use this cautiously, because Frayling leaves its research status unresolved. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, pp. 4–5]`
- **Communicable knowledge:** knowledge that can be conveyed beyond the artefact through report, diary, exposition, or other explicit form; this becomes a crucial threshold term in Frayling’s account. `[@Frayling1993ResearchArtDesign, p. 5]`
