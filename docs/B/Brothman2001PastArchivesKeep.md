# Position + moment (2–4 lines)
Brothman writes in early critical archival theory, at a moment when archivists were increasingly rethinking evidence, memory, organizational knowledge, and the records continuum. He is responding both to older records-life-cycle thinking and to newer continuum ideas, while drawing on philosophy, memory studies, cognitive science, and information technology discourse. His problem-space is the long-term value of records, especially electronic records, and how archivists might justify that value more forcefully in contemporary organizations. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 48–52]`
**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):** This text is strongest when read not as a general metaphorical celebration of memory, but as a careful attempt to redefine the “past” that archives preserve. It is especially useful because it shifts the archive away from passive custody and toward active present-centred cognition. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 50–52, 79–80]`

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)
They try to distinguish the past of memory from the past of history by rethinking archival temporality in order to argue that archival records can remain pragmatically, organizationally, and cognitively active in the present.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Brothman argues that the records continuum is more compatible with memory than the life-cycle model because it allows records to be simultaneously current, regulatory, and historical rather than sequentially moving out of use.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** In the first section he says that the life cycle has effectively relegated archival records to a “marginal role” by treating them as non-current or inactive, whereas the continuum allows records to serve business, corporate memory, and social memory “contemporaneously – and presumably indefinitely.” He also notes McKemmish’s formulation that continuum thinkers take “current, regulatory and historical perspectives on recordkeeping simultaneously, not sequentially.” `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 52–58]`

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)
- **Past of memory:** a present-centred, practical past in which archived material becomes part of active social or organizational cognition rather than remaining a distant historical object. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 50, 64–66]`
- **Historical past / practical past:** Brothman’s adaptation of Oakeshott’s distinction, useful for separating archival use aimed at objective historical narration from archival use aimed at present knowledge and action. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 50, 60–65]`
- **Actualization:** going beyond preserving access to actively constructing conditions under which archival records become usable in present work; I will use this as a key method term for archive activation. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 76–79]`
- **Memory-smart organizations:** organizations that can exploit long-past experience to improve how they handle present issues; I will use this as a useful shorthand when describing the pragmatic case for archival reactivation. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 52–53]`
- **Records as cognitive artifacts:** Brothman’s claim that records are not only evidential or legal artifacts but also materials for cognition and knowledge formation; I will use this as a bridge from archival theory into your computational strand. `[@Brothman2001PastArchivesKeep, pp. 52, 79]`
