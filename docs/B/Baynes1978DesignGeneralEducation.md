# Position + moment (2–4 lines)
This is a Royal College of Art DEU report produced for the Secretary of State for Education and Science after a two-year enquiry begun in 1974 under a 1973 contract. It is not a single-author essay but a state-facing research document that combines terms of reference, team structure, phases of work, and invited supporting papers from outside contributors. That makes it especially valuable as evidence of DDR/DEU as an institutional apparatus for design research rather than just a set of individual ideas. `[@Baynes1978DesignGeneralEducation, pp. 1, 3, 5, 9]`  
**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):** This source complicates any neat story that design education simply emerged as a consensual good. The supporting papers show contest over integration, problem solving, art’s status, curriculum structure, and teacher training, while the team list itself raises questions about distributed labour, including women’s roles, that an Archer-centred history can obscure. `[@Baynes1978DesignGeneralEducation, pp. 5, 13–21, 25–33]`

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)
They try to legitimise design in general education by structuring it as a national research and development enquiry with explicit phases, user requirements, model-building, and evaluation in order to produce a transferable knowledge base for curriculum change.

## Claim 1
- **Claim (plain):** The report frames design education as a national research problem to be investigated systematically, not just as a pedagogic enthusiasm.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The foreword states that the four-part report is the outcome of a two-year enquiry begun in January 1974 in response to a Department of Education and Science contract. The terms of reference define the general aim as bringing about “a wider acceptance of design studies and visual education throughout education” and achieving “a level of design awareness in the general community analogous to literacy or numeracy.” The programme of work is then broken into four phases: analysis, examination of user requirements, development, and evaluation. `[@Baynes1978DesignGeneralEducation, pp. 1, 3]`

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** The report shows that “design in education” was contested from the start, especially around integration, problem solving, and the relation between art and design.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** George Williams’s paper records anxiety among art teachers about the “Design Bandwaggon,” the renaming of art posts as design posts, and the risk that integrated design departments would become empire-building exercises or barriers to wider curricular relationships. He concludes that art and craft teachers need much more information on the scope, jargon, and philosophy of design, plus urgent in-service training. The SEA response likewise warns against a new movement towards problem-solving being abused by ill-informed teachers, opposes materials that are not open-ended, and argues that any understanding of art/design education must recognise a continuum of modes rather than collapse everything into a single design package. `[@Baynes1978DesignGeneralEducation, pp. 13, 20–21, 25–33]`
- **Warrant (my words):** This is important because it stops the report being read as simple institutional consensus. The DEU included disagreement inside its own documentary machinery, which means design education was being negotiated in public and through research, not merely rolled out from above.
- **So what for my thesis:** This report helps me treat contested design knowledge as something built into DDR/DEU’s own documentary record, rather than something I have to import retrospectively.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** The report reveals DEU as a distributed labour structure, which opens a route into the gender-gap question as well as the problem of who gets remembered.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The team list names Archer, Langdon, and Baynes, but also John Harahan, John Bullous, Malcolm Fletton, Walter Parish, Eileen Adams, Deirdre Clark, and Henrietta Ryott as project secretary, with additional members and a separate editorial team. The introduction explains that Part Three includes papers by team members on aspects of the research that could not be fully covered elsewhere. In practice, the scanned contents bear this out: Phil Roberts, John Harahan, and Malcolm Fletton are visibly carrying substantive analytical work in the volume, while the wider membership list indicates a broader labour base than the canonical names alone suggest. `[@Baynes1978DesignGeneralEducation, pp. 5, 9, 35–43, 75–90, 91–109]`
