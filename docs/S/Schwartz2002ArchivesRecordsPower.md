# Position + moment

Published in 2002 as the guest editors' introduction to thematic issues of *Archival Science* on “archives, records, and power,” the article explicitly challenges the professional myth of archival impartiality, neutrality and objectivity. It situates archival practice inside postmodern, feminist, memory and identity debates and argues that both users and archivists need to confront the power relations embodied in record creation, selection, description, preservation and access. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 1–2]

# The author’s main move

Schwartz and Cook argue that archives are socially constructed sites in which power operates through record creation, preservation, appraisal, description, access and interpretation; archivists therefore participate in shaping social memory and identity and cannot credibly treat themselves as passive, neutral custodians. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 1–19]

**Claim.** Archives are active sites of social power rather than passive storehouses. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook reject the view of archives as neutral repositories and argue that archives, records and archivists participate in shaping memory, identity and accountability. **Evidence.** Pages 1–2 state that archives are established and used within power relations, that archivists reshape the archive through appraisal, description, preservation and use, and that archival power should be opened to debate and transparent accountability. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 1–2 state that archives are established and used within power relations, that archivists reshape the archive through appraisal, description, preservation and use, and that archival power should be opened to debate and transparent accountability. **Researcher inference.** The DDR archive should be analysed not only for what records say but for how archival processes structure which traces become visible, legible and reusable. **Warrant.** If archival institutions and professionals select, organise and mediate records, then later historical knowledge is conditioned by those interventions. **Boundary.** The existence of archival power does not prove that every archival decision was consciously political or intentionally exclusionary. **Consequence.** DDR claims about marginality or visibility must identify the concrete archival mechanism involved rather than invoke “power” generically. **Practice cross-check.** Distinguish source content, repository metadata, digitisation boundary and research-derived interpretation in the DDR evidence model.
## Claim 2

**Claim.** Archives are social constructs whose frameworks shape what can become memory. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook argue that archives originate in the information needs and values of the rulers, governments, businesses, associations and individuals who create and maintain them. **Evidence.** Pages 3–5 describe archives as social-intellectual frameworks in which decisions about what to record, preserve and privilege shape collective memory; even individual documents reflect creator purpose, audience and broader legal, organisational, social and cultural contexts. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 3–5 describe archives as social-intellectual frameworks in which decisions about what to record, preserve and privilege shape collective memory; even individual documents reflect creator purpose, audience and broader legal, organisational, social and cultural contexts. **Researcher inference.** DDR records should be treated as situated products of institutional and project activity rather than transparent containers of historical fact. **Warrant.** Documentary survival begins with historically specific acts of recording and preservation before later archivists or researchers ever encounter the material. **Boundary.** Social construction does not mean records are arbitrary or incapable of supporting strong historical claims. **Consequence.** Source criticism should ask who created a DDR record, for what purpose, for which audience and within what institutional conditions. **Practice cross-check.** Evidence cards should preserve creator, date, repository, record type and surrounding project context wherever available.
## Claim 3

**Claim.** Archival power operates before, during and after records enter the archive. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook locate archival power in record creation, naming, classification, appraisal, selection, description, preservation, access and later use. **Evidence.** Pages 5–6 and 13–15 enumerate these stages and show how both records creators and archivists shape what becomes knowable. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 5–6 and 13–15 enumerate these stages and show how both records creators and archivists shape what becomes knowable. **Researcher inference.** DDR archival mediation cannot be reduced to catalogue description alone; it includes upstream recordkeeping and downstream access/interface decisions. **Warrant.** Historical legibility is cumulative: every stage can preserve, suppress, foreground or reorder relationships. **Boundary.** The specific effect of any one stage must be demonstrated in the DDR case rather than assumed from the general model. **Consequence.** The thesis should distinguish non-creation, non-survival, appraisal, sparse description, digitisation exclusion and retrieval failure as different evidential conditions. **Practice cross-check.** Use scoped missingness to state where a defined DDR evidence surface fails and at which stage the limit can or cannot be identified.
## Claim 4

**Claim.** Archival neutrality is a professional myth that obscures responsibility. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook argue that archivists have historically portrayed themselves as neutral, objective and passive, even though their decisions shape the archive. **Evidence.** Pages 1–2 and 5–6 state that denial of archival power prevents accountability; pp. 9–13 further challenge positivist notions of objectivity and use situated knowledge to recast archival interpretation as power-sensitive. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 1–2 and 5–6 state that denial of archival power prevents accountability; pp. 9–13 further challenge positivist notions of objectivity and use situated knowledge to recast archival interpretation as power-sensitive. **Researcher inference.** The thesis's own corpus construction, metadata mapping, retrieval settings and interface design should be documented as situated research interventions rather than presented as neutral technical operations. **Warrant.** Responsibility becomes possible only when the decisions shaping archival evidence are made visible. **Boundary.** Rejecting neutrality does not eliminate standards of evidence, transparency or professional discipline. **Consequence.** The project should pursue accountable rather than supposedly view-from-nowhere archival computation. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve corpus rules, model versions, retrieval parameters, source bindings and researcher-defined categories.
## Claim 5

**Claim.** Archival power is also representational power over identity and collective memory. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook argue that archives validate identity narratives while archival selection and description can privilege some groups and marginalise others. **Evidence.** Pages 15–18 connect archives to identity politics and discuss the systemic exclusion of women and other marginalised groups from archival memory; they also insist archivists should search for missing voices while avoiding simplistic rescue narratives. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 15–18 connect archives to identity politics and discuss the systemic exclusion of women and other marginalised groups from archival memory; they also insist archivists should search for missing voices while avoiding simplistic rescue narratives. **Researcher inference.** DDR feminist analysis should examine how records, role labels, project attribution and descriptive choices affect the later legibility of women's and supporting actors' contributions. **Warrant.** Identity and historical recognition depend partly on whether documentary traces survive and are made discoverable as evidence. **Boundary.** Absence from the DDR archive cannot itself prove sexism, racism, classism or deliberate exclusion; the authors explicitly warn that indifference, ignorance and other mechanisms may also be involved. **Consequence.** Feminist archive work should investigate evidence of mechanism and avoid turning sparse survival into a predetermined political explanation. **Practice cross-check.** Compare named authorship, acknowledgements, staff lists, role metadata and oral testimony for DDR women without equating those evidence forms.
## Claim 6

**Claim.** Archival power is not monolithic: records created by dominant institutions can also support resistance and counter-reading. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook qualify their power argument by noting chaos, inconsistency, subversion and the possibility of reading mainstream records “against the grain.” **Evidence.** Pages 14–15 explicitly state that archives are not only about control; privileged records can contain traces that support oppositional readings and marginal actors may create records for future visibility. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 14–15 explicitly state that archives are not only about control; privileged records can contain traces that support oppositional readings and marginal actors may create records for future visibility. **Researcher inference.** DDR records created by senior staff may still contain indirect traces of overlooked participants, labour and disagreement. **Warrant.** Documentary intention does not exhaust documentary evidential potential. **Boundary.** Indirect traces are weaker than direct evidence and can be over-read. **Consequence.** Relationship reconstruction should surface indirect evidence while preserving its evidential status and uncertainty. **Practice cross-check.** Treat acknowledgements, copied correspondence, staff lists and incidental mentions as distinct from formal authorship or documented action.
## Claim 7

**Claim.** New media and electronic access can both redistribute and intensify archival power. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook argue that electronic records and networked access create risks of privileging only certain information while also enabling access for geographically and socially dispersed publics. **Evidence.** Page 15 states that information technology can empower users through broader access but simultaneously amplifies archival mediation through system architecture, online selection and virtual presentation. **Evidence-supported claim.** Page 15 states that information technology can empower users through broader access but simultaneously amplifies archival mediation through system architecture, online selection and virtual presentation. **Researcher inference.** DDR computational interfaces can widen access while also creating new ranking, visibility and authority effects. **Warrant.** Digital access is not neutral redistribution; technical design itself becomes part of archival mediation. **Boundary.** The article predates contemporary embeddings, LLMs and retrieval-augmented inference. **Consequence.** Later AI-specific literature is needed to govern computational behaviour, but Schwartz and Cook supply the archival reason to scrutinise interface authority. **Practice cross-check.** Keep semantic views, retrieval rankings and generated synthesis linked visibly to their underlying evidence and scope.

**Claim.** Critical archival practice should pursue plurality without pretending that any archive can be complete. **Author claim.** Schwartz and Cook call for archivists to seek missing voices and multiple tellings while recognising that other stories could always have been chosen. **Evidence.** Pages 17–19 recommend fuller attention to multiplicity, warn against romanticising the marginalised, and conclude that archival meaning is layered by creators, managers, archivists and users. **Evidence-supported claim.** Pages 17–19 recommend fuller attention to multiplicity, warn against romanticising the marginalised, and conclude that archival meaning is layered by creators, managers, archivists and users. **Researcher inference.** The DDR thesis should aim for accountable plurality rather than a definitive reconstruction of the department. **Warrant.** Critical archival knowledge remains partial even when it improves representation and context. **Boundary.** Plurality does not mean abandoning discrimination among stronger and weaker evidence. **Consequence.** Final synthesis should preserve ambiguity and unresolved contestation instead of forcing closure. **Practice cross-check.** Maintain alternative evidence routes, source-status distinctions and explicit limits in the DDR research instrument.
# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Archival power:** power exercised through record creation, preservation, appraisal, description, access and interpretation, shaping memory and identity. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 1–6, 13–18]
- **Archives as social constructs:** archives understood as products of the needs, values and frameworks of the societies and institutions that create and maintain them. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 3–5]
- **Archival mediation:** the cumulative intervention of creators, recordkeepers, archivists and later users in shaping how records are preserved, organised and interpreted. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 1–6, 13–19]
- **Reading against the grain:** using records created within dominant structures to recover indirect, oppositional or marginal traces not foregrounded by their creators. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 14–15]
- **Power-sensitive conversation:** the authors' adaptation of situated-knowledge thinking to archives, in which creators, keepers and users continually interpret records within relations of power. [@Schwartz2002ArchivesRecordsPower, pp. 11–13]

# My response

Schwartz and Cook give this thesis its clearest canonical reason for refusing to treat the DDR archive as a neutral reservoir of facts. Their argument is not that archival evidence is unreliable by definition; it is that evidence reaches the researcher through layered acts of creation, survival, selection, description and access. That distinction is crucial. It allows the thesis to retain strong evidential standards while making archival mediation itself analytically visible. It also prevents a simplistic politics of absence: the authors repeatedly acknowledge complexity, counter-reading and the danger of romanticising marginality.

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** Schwartz and Cook provide a broad critical theory of archives, records and power; they do not provide empirical evidence about the RCA's DDR archive or the motives behind any particular absence, description or archival decision.

**Risk if misused:** The language of archival power could become totalising, allowing every gap or classification to be interpreted as deliberate exclusion. The thesis should instead identify specific mechanisms and retain “unknown” where the evidence cannot establish cause.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Schwartz and Cook form the canonical hinge for the Critical archival theory cluster. Duranti historicises description; Bearman reconceives documentation around activity and evidence; Cook's earlier paradigm history explains the move toward context and process; Ketelaar develops activation and memory; Caswell, Punzalan and Sangwand make critical archival work explicitly emancipatory; Carter and Moss/Thomas differentiate silence and missingness; Cifor and Wood connect archival power to feminist praxis; Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan show how digitisation can reproduce or focalise absence; and Jaillant/Rees and Jaillant/Aske translate archival responsibility into AI-era trust, access and infrastructure. For DDR, the combined framework is therefore not “archives are biased” but something more precise: records and archival systems actively mediate historical legibility, and computational activation is defensible only when those mediations, evidential statuses and limits remain visible.
