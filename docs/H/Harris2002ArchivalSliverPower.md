# Position + moment

Harris writes from direct involvement in South Africa’s transition from apartheid to democracy and from archival investigations into state-record destruction. He uses that extreme case to challenge positivist archival assumptions more generally, while explicitly warning against simply generalising from exceptional circumstances. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 63–66]

# The author’s main move

The essay replaces the metaphor of archive-as-mirror with the “archival sliver”: records represent only fragments of documentary and social memory, and those fragments are further shaped by creation, destruction, appraisal, preservation, description, access and use. Archivists and researchers are not outside these processes; they participate in constructing what becomes socially memorable. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 64–65, 82–85]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** The archival record is radically partial even before archival selection begins. **Author claim.** Harris argues that the documentary record is only a “sliver” of social memory and that the archival record is a further sliver of that documentary record. **Evidence.** He describes deliberate and inadvertent destruction by creators and managers, selection by archivists and the finite life of records, culminating in the formulation “a sliver of a sliver of a sliver.” [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 64–65] **Evidence-supported claim.** Surviving archives cannot be assumed to provide complete coverage of past events or experience. **Researcher inference.** DDR missingness must be stated as a limit of the surviving/digitised evidence surface rather than evidence that an event, role or response did not occur. **Warrant.** Multiple selection and loss processes intervene before the researcher sees the archive. **Boundary.** Harris’s vivid formulation arises from a context involving systematic state destruction; not every archival gap has such an intentional cause. **Consequence.** The thesis should make absence claims explicitly scoped and avoid converting non-retrieval into historical non-existence. **Practice cross-check.** Use scoped missingness language in UAT and thesis writing where the defined DDR corpus cannot establish a proposition.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Archives do not mirror reality because recording itself changes the processes from which records emerge. **Author claim.** Harris offers three challenges to the mirror metaphor: historical reality is irrecoverable in its uniqueness; process is shaped by the act of recording; and records operate through creators, managers, archivists and researchers. **Evidence.** The essay states that records “reflect” only in a fractured and complicit way and that all these conduits participate in how records feed into social memory. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 65] **Evidence-supported claim.** A record is part of a historical process rather than an external transparent representation of it. **Researcher inference.** DDR reports, project files and catalogue records should be read as products of institutional activity with their own purposes and conditions of production. **Warrant.** The act of documenting can shape what is done, retained and later knowable. **Boundary.** This does not mean records are unusable as evidence. **Consequence.** Source criticism must ask why a document exists, what work it performed and through which archival processes it survived. **Practice cross-check.** Distinguish catalogue association, recorded action, inferred intent and later testimony in evidence packets.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Archives are both expressions and instruments of power. **Author claim.** Harris shows how apartheid memory institutions, bureaucratic recordkeeping, secrecy and destruction helped normalise and sustain prevailing relations of power. **Evidence.** He describes state-controlled archives and other memory institutions as carrying narratives and silences that legitimised apartheid, while bureaucratic records documented and administered racial classification, surveillance and control. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 69–70] **Evidence-supported claim.** Archival systems can participate in governance and social ordering rather than merely preserving evidence about them. **Researcher inference.** DDR archival organisation and description may similarly shape historical visibility, though without implying equivalence to the apartheid case. **Warrant.** Selection, classification and access influence what institutions and researchers can know and narrate. **Boundary.** The political intensity and coercive function of apartheid archives are historically specific. **Consequence.** The thesis should analyse archive structure as an interpretative condition while avoiding inflated moral equivalence. **Practice cross-check.** Examine how DDR catalogue keywords, staff naming and project descriptions amplify some actors and obscure others.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Formal access can remain exclusionary when institutions do not address structural barriers. **Author claim.** Harris notes that South African public archives were nominally open and free while black South Africans formed only a small proportion of users because of educational, linguistic, geographic and service-design barriers. **Evidence.** He describes outreach and service provision as oriented toward white users and later white academic/genealogical constituencies. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 71] **Evidence-supported claim.** Availability and equitable accessibility are not the same condition. **Researcher inference.** Digitising DDR records or exposing them through an interface does not by itself guarantee meaningful access or balanced discoverability. **Warrant.** Access is shaped by institutional design, language, description and user assumptions. **Boundary.** The social barriers in Harris’s case differ greatly from those of the DDR research environment. **Consequence.** The thesis should distinguish technical availability from legibility and research usability. **Practice cross-check.** Evaluate whether the DDR instrument exposes context and uncertainty rather than only returning technically accessible search results.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Archivists are active shapers of social memory rather than impartial custodians. **Author claim.** Harris identifies this as the leitmotif of post-apartheid archival transformation discourse and connects it to demands for representivity, transparency and accountability. **Evidence.** He states that archivists are “active documenters of society and shapers of social memory” and argues that archives must be accountable because of this shaping role. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 77] **Evidence-supported claim.** Professional archival choices participate in what becomes visible, preserved and interpretable. **Researcher inference.** The DDR catalogue and subsequent digital research instrument both belong inside the history of mediation rather than outside it. **Warrant.** Selection and description are knowledge-producing interventions. **Boundary.** Agency is distributed; archivists do not control all record creation, survival or later interpretation. **Consequence.** The thesis should make its own mediation visible rather than position the research instrument as a neutral window onto DDR. **Practice cross-check.** Document corpus releases, modelling decisions and interface changes as part of the examined research state.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Appraisal creates archival value rather than merely discovering value already present in records. **Author claim.** Harris criticises the notion that appraisers assemble a coherent reflection of reality and argues that their values, policies, interactions and judgements shape what becomes archival. **Evidence.** In the conclusion he states that the appraiser is not simply identifying archival value but creating it, and that appraisal reports themselves should be treated as records with a particular ontological status. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 84] **Evidence-supported claim.** Appraisal is part of the provenance of the evidence surface available to later researchers. **Researcher inference.** DDR digitisation and corpus inclusion criteria similarly create a research evidence surface without becoming equivalent to institutional archival appraisal. **Warrant.** Decisions about what enters a corpus affect the claims the instrument can support. **Boundary.** Research-corpus construction is not the same institutional function as archival appraisal. **Consequence.** Corpus selection should be documented as a methodological intervention and never mistaken for the total archive. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve inclusion/exclusion criteria and release hashes for the PID-backed DDR corpus.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** Converting oral testimony into material custody can reproduce marginalisation if the fluidity and status of orality are ignored. **Author claim.** Harris criticises oral-history programmes that treat recorded testimony merely as source material for historiography, fail to understand orality’s fluidity and refuse to engage orality as a form of archive. **Evidence.** He argues that such practices can further disempower marginalised voices rather than automatically rescuing them. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 84] **Evidence-supported claim.** Recording and archiving testimony do not neutralise the power relations involved in representing voice. **Researcher inference.** DDR oral histories need their own evidential and narrative integrity rather than being reduced to searchable quotations that merely fill documentary gaps. **Warrant.** Materialisation changes the form, ownership and interpretative context of oral testimony. **Boundary.** Harris’s discussion is brief and embedded in a wider critique; Abrams provides the more detailed oral-history methodology. **Consequence.** Oral testimony should be cross-read with documents without being subordinated to them. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve interview provenance, larger narrative context and testimonial status when excerpts enter computational retrieval.

## Claim 8

**Claim.** Critical archival contextualisation should open meaning rather than close it. **Author claim.** Harris criticises finding aids and other archival interventions that aim to resolve mystery and “detain” meaning; he calls instead for contextualisation that reveals multiple layers of construction, including the archivist’s own layer. **Evidence.** His concluding pages describe archival records as open-ended constructions and call on archivists to disclose complicity, welcome otherness and recognise every knowledge-making move as an exercise of power. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 84–85] **Evidence-supported claim.** Archival mediation is more accountable when it exposes its own construction and leaves room for competing interpretations. **Researcher inference.** DDR computational synthesis should surface relations provisionally and retain ambiguity instead of presenting one generated answer as closure. **Warrant.** A system that conceals mediation turns interpretation into authority. **Boundary.** “Opening the archive” is a normative orientation rather than a precise technical protocol. **Consequence.** The research instrument should make unsupported claims harder to produce and keep alternative readings available. **Practice cross-check.** Use source-linked synthesis, ambiguity retention and scoped missingness as design constraints.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Archival sliver →** the surviving archival record understood as a multiply selected, incomplete and mediated fragment of documentary and social memory. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 64–65]
- **Archival value →** not simply an inherent property discovered by appraisal but a value partly produced through appraisal decisions and their institutional context. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, p. 84]
- **Opening the archive →** contextualising records in ways that reveal layers of construction and permit competing meanings rather than closing interpretation through authoritative narration. [@Harris2002ArchivalSliverPower, pp. 84–85]

# My response

Harris gives the thesis its clearest reason not to confuse archival activation with recovery. The archive is already a selected and constructed sliver before my computational work begins; my own corpus, retrieval and interface then add further layers. The methodological obligation is therefore not to pretend mediation can be removed, but to make each layer inspectable and to constrain what I claim from it.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** The DDR archive can be activated only as a mediated and incomplete evidence surface: its value lies not in recovering an unbroken past but in making the surviving layers of record, description, testimony and interpretation critically legible.

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** Harris’s empirical case is apartheid and post-apartheid South Africa, a context of systematic oppression, secrecy and deliberate record destruction that should not be analogised casually to DDR.

**Risk:** The “sliver” metaphor could become an excuse for epistemic pessimism. Harris does not make archives useless; he makes their use conditional on acknowledging selection, power and mediation.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Harris supplies the critical proposition that archival description, appraisal and use are interventions in memory rather than neutral conduits. ISAD(G) makes those interventions operationally visible by formalising hierarchy, creator, archival history, appraisal information and description control. Abrams extends the same problem to oral testimony, where memory and interviewer interaction create another mediated trace. Aske and Giardinetti then show how inherited description enters computational systems as data. Together these sources support the thesis’s layered evidential model: source, metadata, testimony and synthesis must remain distinguishable because each is produced through different mediations. What still has to be demonstrated against DDR is which historical claims survive that separation and where ambiguity or missingness must remain unresolved.
