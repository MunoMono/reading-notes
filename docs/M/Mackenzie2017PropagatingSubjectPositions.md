# Thesis job

**Contribution to my primary question:** Mackenzie investigates the *subject positions* produced by machine-learning operations, apparatuses, benchmark measures and institutions. This brings a further level of accountability to my DDR instrument: I must ask which people and systems are positioned to produce a relationship, interpret a document, certify an answer or be made invisible by the resulting account. Source-attributed archival evidence, retrieved relevance, machine-generated prose and my own judgement do not have interchangeable speaking authority.

**Contribution to my secondary question:** If revisiting DDR research is to inform contemporary practice, I should also inspect what current computational design does to the figure of the researcher, the historian, the archivist and the contributor. The chapter does not tell me which historical DDR theories retain value; that historical and normative comparison is still mine to demonstrate.

**Why read this chapter now:** Chapters 1–7 have traced operations of vectorisation, function finding, probabilisation, differentiation and the materialisation of objects. Chapter 8 turns toward **subjects who perform, interpret, judge and are judged by these operations**. It joins the critical computational approaches lens to a carefully delimited feminist/situated question about credit, labour, visibility and agency.

**My benchmark:** (1) I will use the argument when I can identify, in one versioned DDR inquiry, the *different agents and decision points* responsible for digitisation, classification, retrieval, synthesis, source validation and historical interpretation. (2) I will assess the evidential adequacy of each historical statement separately from model-score improvement, human competition ranking or interface fluency; use difficult, source-contrary and bounded-missingness cases as well as successful answers.

# Position, argument and material

Mackenzie begins with an ethnographic vignette: in a Manchester software company around 2002, a neural-network specialist named Vlad sat apart from the team building a call-centre knowledge-management product (p. 179). He contrasts that specialist role with Hilary Mason's 2012 appeal to developers who combined infrastructure, mathematics and human-behaviour expertise, an emergent subject formed at an intersection of formerly separate skills (pp. 179–181). Drawing on Foucault's idea of the **subject position** and Lucy Suchman's account of human–machine boundaries, he asks how operations and statements assign places to human and non-human machine learners (pp. 181–182, 197).

The chapter follows artificial neural networks from 1980s connectionist work and back-propagation through handwritten-digit recognition, code examples, neural-network diagrams and 2010s competitive data science (pp. 182–199). It then considers Kaggle-style competitions as ranking and examination regimes in which a platform sets problems, scores predictions, ranks practitioners and feeds the scores into subsequent attempts (pp. 200–204). A plankton-recognition competition supplies a detailed case of collaborative model building, specialist hardware and uneven access to computational resources (pp. 204–206). The conclusion argues that human and machine learner positions are repeatedly adjusted through testing, error and infrastructures (pp. 207–208).

**Main move:** Mackenzie refuses to locate all intelligence or authorship either in an extraordinary human expert or in an autonomous algorithm. He examines how the apparent authority to speak is *distributed and rearranged* among technical operations, material resources, the stories told about models, institutions of comparison and the labour of people whom those arrangements privilege or marginalise (particularly pp. 181–182, 186, 197–200, 203, 207–208).

**Historical/technical boundary:** These are cases and institutional conditions documented up to 2017, not evidence about contemporary model training or the 2026 DDR RAI deployment. Back-propagation in a trained neural network is also **not the same operation** as returning UAT feedback to a software developer or curator: only a bounded methodological analogy is warranted.

# Nine critical claims

## Claim 1 — The 'machine learner subject' is a position produced through operations, not a single person or machine

**Author claim:** Mackenzie uses the Foucauldian notion of subject position to investigate who can make statements and perform operations within machine learning, and argues that the position is distributed rather than a unified interior intelligence. **Evidence-supported claim:** The chapter states that machine learner subjects are reassigned as neural networks traverse different knowledge and infrastructure settings (pp. 181–182, 186). The close discussion of Foucault on p. 197 establishes a circular relationship between the *subject of an operation* and the *subject of its statement*; Mackenzie applies that relationship to machine-learning transformations and the claims they produce (p. 197). **Warrant:** A statement of computational success gains its speaking position partly from the operations and conventions that allow it to be made and repeated. **My inference:** A DDR answer should identify the source author or witness, the archive/catalogue record, the retriever's relevance proposal, the generator's textual synthesis and the researcher's interpretative decision as different forms of contribution and authority. **Boundary:** Foucault's 'subject position' is an analytic description of how statements can be made; it does not give an LLM personhood or make a machine a historically accountable eyewitness. Nor does this chapter establish that DDR's actual user interface enforces these distinctions. **Consequence:** In the thesis and instrument, represent source evidence, testimony, generated claims and researcher synthesis distinctly. **Practice cross-check:** Trace one production DDR answer to its cited PID documents, model output, provenance guard and human adjudication; **TODO (record test question, versions and passage IDs)**. ## Claim 2 — Machine learning redistributes professional expertise rather than simply replacing specialists

## Claim 3 — The 'mind of a neural network' is a consequential rhetoric of technical agency

**Author claim:** Mackenzie treats the language of minds and machine seeing as part of the changing distribution of human–machine positions rather than a harmless flourish. **Evidence-supported claim:** On p. 182 he examines Hinton, Osindero and Teh's use of generatively reconstructed digit images to claim that observers could look into a network's 'mind'; he notes that the authors explicitly disavow a purely metaphorical use of that term. He then observes that 'looking' typically means plotting or diagramming a model-produced pattern, while neural networks are described alternately through cognition and vectorised infrastructure (pp. 182–183). **Warrant:** A rendered output can make a technical transformation appear like an autonomous, mentally endowed observer. ## Claim 4 — Neural-network capabilities depend on infrastructures and inherited techniques as well as 'breakthrough' algorithms

**Author claim:** Mackenzie contests any history that attributes the changing fortunes of neural networks solely to algorithmic genius, instead following how connectionist models, semiconductor parallelism, new data forms and corporate infrastructures come together (pp. 183–186, 190–192). **Evidence-supported claim:** He connects a 1985 connectionist paper to VLSI architectures (p. 184), reproduces the **original report's documentation page** as **Figure 8.2** (p. 185), and discusses later GPUs/data-centre scale (p. 186). He explicitly argues that the underlying back-propagation and gradient ideas had not been radically reinvented between their 1980s forms and the 2010s neural-net resurgence (p. 191). **Figure 8.1** (p. 183) also shows uneven disciplinary attention to neural-network techniques over time. **Warrant:** A capability becomes available through accumulated technical and institutional conditions; performance cannot be explained entirely by a timeless algorithm name or by a charismatic individual. **My inference:** The DDR methodology should record the model version, corpus scope, source pipeline, dependencies, compute conditions, iteration and evaluation together, explaining why the system could do what it did *at that stage of research*. **Boundary:** Figure 8.2 shows a 1985 report with a US Office of Naval Research funding field; the document is evidence of that particular research setting, not proof of any continuous military determination of contemporary DDR AI. This 2017 genealogy also does not explain the architecture or training provenance of an uninspected current Qwen or embedding model. **Consequence:** Describe the actual computational research instrument as a versioned, collaborative system, avoiding single-agent histories of both software and DDR. **Practice cross-check:** Locate the DDR corpus and deployment receipts, model-selection rationale and significant UAT revisions; **TODO (source files, SHAs and versions)**. ## Claim 5 — A seemingly simple neural-network demonstration hides human classifications and learned transformations

**Author claim:** Mackenzie shows how layered networks derive internal features and how examples of neural-network code carry already defined class variables, preprocessing decisions, error functions and architecture choices (pp. 191–196). **Evidence-supported claim:** **Equation 8.1** (p. 191) and its discussion (p. 192) diagram layer-by-layer derived representations; **Listing 8.1** (p. 193) constructs a model of Titanic survival after recoding categorical source data such as sex and embarkation into numerical indicators. **Figure 8.3**, occupying printed p. 196, depicts three hidden units, input labels, many weighted connections and a single 'survived' output. ## Claim 6 — Benchmarks establish publicly visible *comparative performance*, not general authority to interpret a domain

**Author claim:** Mackenzie traces the elevation of handwritten-digit recognition to a testing ground where classifiers and their makers compete through error scores, helping establish a field of machine-learning expertise (pp. 187–189). **Evidence-supported claim:** He notes the repeated use of digit datasets and model comparisons (pp. 187–188), including the striking fact that earlier high-performing neural networks depended on considerable *handcrafting* to reduce errors (p. 188). In the later competition analysis, training data, withheld labels and leaderboard ranking become central to the public visibility of results (**Figure 8.4**, p. 202; explanation pp. 201–202). **Warrant:** A benchmark can make an improvement recognisable and repeatable while privileging the target it scores over other qualities of understanding or expert labour. **My inference:** DDR UAT should not be presented simply as a leaderboard of successful answers. A PASS must state which historical propositions the cited passages support, with negative and ambiguous evidence preserved; performance against the frozen question set cannot be assumed to establish universal interpretative competence. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's performance examples concern supervised prediction against known labels; historical relationship questions can lack agreed ground truth. DDR production-only UAT is a researcher-governed acceptance process, not gradient descent, a Kaggle competition or a direct measure of a historical population. **Consequence:** Keep retrieval adequacy, provenance admissibility, claim faithfulness and historian judgement analytically distinct, and explain how test questions were chosen. **Practice cross-check:** Compare one passing DDR result and one rejected/ambiguous result against the source passages and the independent UAT rubric; **TODO (test IDs, expected evidence, published answer, human disposition)**. ## Claim 7 — Ranking infrastructures assign work, incentives and epistemic positions to people

**Author claim:** Mackenzie extends Foucauldian examination from model scoring to the institutional setting of competitive platforms; participants are ranked, recruited, rewarded and encouraged to reshape their practice around the score (pp. 200–204). **Evidence-supported claim:** He describes the Kaggle 'download–build–submit' loop, visible rankings and recruitment connections (pp. 201–202), while **Table 8.1** (p. 204) lists competitions across health, education, transport, finance and other domains with monetary rewards. On p. 203 he applies Foucault's notion of the examination as a conjunction of **power and knowledge**, a form that produces visibility and normalising judgement. **Warrant:** Evaluation criteria do not merely record results; in institutional settings they can motivate repeated behaviour, legitimise expertise and reorganise authority. **My inference:** When I define what counts as 'PASS' in the DDR instrument, I also define which kinds of response improvement are rewarded or refused. The historical research question should therefore govern my UAT rubric, not merely a desire for higher completion rates or smoother prose. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's platform comparison with neural-network back-propagation is *conceptual*, not a literal claim that human competitors are differentiable neural nodes. Evidence about Kaggle in 2015 cannot establish the current platform's practices. Nor does his Foucauldian analogy prove that DDR research software exerts the same disciplinary power as prize-based competitions. **Consequence:** Critically document the provenance of evaluation criteria, who adjudicates them and how improvements can trade off against source-level reliability. **Practice cross-check:** Review successive DDR UAT rubrics and remediation decisions to find an instance where an apparently better synthesis was refused because it exceeded evidence; **TODO (versioned UAT packet, decision and reviewer rationale)**. ## Claim 8 — Computational advantage is also a distribution of material resources and collective labour

**Author claim:** Through the 2015 National Data Science Bowl's plankton-recognition task, Mackenzie shows modern machine learning as a collective enterprise reliant on specialist computational infrastructure, repeated model training and configuration (pp. 204–206). **Evidence-supported claim:** He describes the winning team of seven graduate/postdoctoral researchers and its layered convolutional models (p. 205), followed by the team's use of high-end GPU hardware, training lasting 24–48 hours and a reported population of around 300 models (p. 206). He explicitly notes that many competitors could not readily access similar compute (p. 206). **Warrant:** Ranking a result as superior may reflect access to time, hardware, software practices and team labour as well as the algorithmic formulation. **My inference:** The DDR research account should acknowledge access to archive resources, OCR work, developer time, model capacity and domain expertise as conditions shaping what the project can know and publish. **Boundary:** These are reported conditions of one 2015 competition; the chapter does not measure hardware access across all participants. It also characterises earlier convolutional layers as 'strictly speaking unsupervised' (p. 205), a broad technical characterisation which the chapter's description alone does not independently establish for every model end-to-end; **TODO (verify independently before adopting as a technical fact)**. More computing power cannot be equated with greater source-grounded historical reliability. **Consequence:** Record resources and labour as research conditions, and report task-specific validation rather than model size as the criterion of success. **Practice cross-check:** Assemble the DDR instrument's model/runtime history, human roles and archive-scanning/processing decisions; **TODO (dated contribution log, tool/model versions and documentary sources)**. ## Claim 9 — Authorship and invisible work cut across both the history of machines and the archive I study

**Author claim:** The chapter makes human–machine subject positions uneven, including unequal recognition among researchers and potentially invisible human work displaced by a story of technical achievement. **Evidence-supported claim:** A footnote on p. 181 explicitly remarks that notable women are outnumbered by men in its illustrative lists of celebrated machine learners; the opening distinguishes a prominent evangelist of machine-learning expertise from an isolated worker tending a central technical component (pp. 179–181). On p. 198, Mackenzie speculates that postal workers' labour may have become less visible as automated digit recognition improved; on pp. 207–208, the chapter returns to the question of who can occupy prestigious or remote positions in the ranking system. **Warrant:** The operational history of a technical system and the public history of credited expertise can differ. Scrutinising that gap is necessary when the object of inquiry is who contributed to knowledge. **My inference:** This is the chapter's most direct bridge to my feminist/situated lens: I should distinguish named authors, documentary contributors, administrative labour, model operators and subsequent commentators when assessing DDR history. Computational prominence or sparse catalogue naming must not stand in for historical authorship. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's gender observation is not a systematic gender audit of the profession; the postal-worker point is explicitly speculative, not proven from labour records; and none of this is direct evidence of women's particular DDR contributions or omissions. Those require original archival documents, institutional records and testimony. **Consequence:** Treat labour attribution and absences as explicit, testable questions with source-specific evidential status. **Practice cross-check:** Select a documented DDR woman or little-credited participant; compare original document credits, staff lists, catalogue descriptions, retrieval results and any oral-history claims, without letting computational association assign intention or authorship; **TODO (person, PIDs, dates and adjudication)**. # Definitions and distinctions for my thesis

- **Subject position (Mackenzie via Foucault):** A position from which statements and operations can be made, grounded in a dispersed formation of practices rather than simply an individual mind or stable professional title (pp. 179–181, 197). **My use:** Specify who or what contributes to each stage of documentary interpretation and which output is qualified to make which claim.
- **Human–machine boundary:** An historically and practically made allocation of agency, work and visibility, discussed through Suchman (p. 181). **My use:** A critical question about DDR's division of labour and attribution; not an assertion of machine legal or moral personhood.
- **Back-propagation:** A training procedure that propagates a measured error through network layers to adjust connection weights (pp. 184, 194, 198–199). **My use:** A technical explanation where relevant to training histories; only a *metaphor* if discussing feedback from UAT or research reflection.
- **Derived features / hidden units:** Intermediary model representations constructed through the interaction of layers and adjusted parameters (pp. 192, 194–196). **My use:** A reminder to distinguish representation made by a model from categories or interpretations attested by records.
- **Generalisation error:** Model predictive error on data beyond its training fit, discussed in competitive evaluation and the chapter's framing (pp. 179, 200–203). **My use:** A precise technical concept, not a synonym for a wrong claim about DDR.
- **Competition as examination:** A platform or setting in which evaluation and ranking simultaneously establish truth-like performance differences and positions of recognised expertise (pp. 200–204). **My use:** An analytic prompt for critically justifying evaluation measures in my practice rather than equating source-based UAT with a public leaderboard.
- **Privileged machine:** Mackenzie's analytic focus on neural nets at the intersection of technical infrastructure, claims of cognition and distributions of agency (pp. 181–182, 190–191). **My use:** Question how interface and narrative may over-credit model activity at the expense of sources, archivists and researchers.
- **Optimism about optimisation:** The tendency for predictive improvement and error reduction to authorise expectations of new capabilities or expertise (pp. 207–208). **My use:** An explicit risk when claiming that every model or feature improvement necessarily advances historical inquiry.

# My critical evaluation

This chapter extends the book's archaeology in an important way: the unit of analysis is no longer only the *object* stabilised through a model, but the **position from which a result is asserted and its authority accepted**. The contrast between Vlad's semi-hidden work and Hilary Mason's celebrated cross-disciplinary programmer is especially effective because it makes 'machine learner' a variable institutional arrangement rather than a job title (pp. 179–181). The opening is substantiated by varied representations. **Figure 8.1** (p. 183) visualises the changing prominence of technical keywords across disciplines; **Figure 8.2** (p. 185) embeds neural-network development in the bureaucratic materiality of a 1985 technical report; **Listing 8.1** (p. 193) exposes the mundane work of encoding Titanic categories and choosing model settings; and **Figure 8.3** (p. 196) pictures hidden units and their layered relations. Finally **Figure 8.4** (p. 202) and **Table 8.1** (p. 204) show the evaluation platform and the real-world tasks, domains and rewards through which positions of expertise are distributed.

Three qualifications matter for an academically defensible use. **First**, neural-network error propagation and platform competitions are analogous feedback architectures only at a very broad level: an individual competitor is not a neural parameter, and numerical leaderboard feedback is not automatic training of a researcher's judgement (pp. 198–204). The account is more convincing as a **critical institutional description** of rankings than as a formal algorithmic equivalence. **Second**, the historically situated narrative foregrounds spectacular named researchers and competition winners, even while it asks about the distribution of work and recognises a gender imbalance (pp. 181, 188–190, 204–206). That tension becomes productive for my feminist reading, but it is not resolved by the chapter's own empirical evidence. **Third**, the talk of viewing a model's 'mind' and of learned representations (pp. 182, 194–195) needs careful handling: showing that a model generates digits or fits labels does not establish thought, historical understanding or competence outside its configured task.

A further technical caution: the Titanic R code is OCR-damaged (p. 193), and the claim that components of the plankton classifier count straightforwardly as supervised or unsupervised (p. 205) is not fully testable from the brief model description. Neither should be used as independent technical evidence without a checked source or implementation. The 2017 discussion does not cover contemporary retrieval-augmented inference or multimodal language-model deployments; the relevance to DDR is conceptual and demands actual research-practice evidence.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** *In the DDR instrument, computational outputs acquire authority through situated relations among documents, catalogues, models, designers and evaluators; a defensible historical claim must therefore reveal not only its cited trace but also the position and procedure from which that claim is made.*

# Boundary and risk

**Boundary:** The chapter concerns neural networks, human practitioners, training and ranking systems principally through cases from the 1980s to 2015; it was published in 2017. No substantive claims about the DDR archive, Qwen, bge-m3, UMAP's current implementation or the actual correctness of DDR UAT are derived from this source.

**Risk:** Treating 'subject position' as a claim of AI sentience; replacing archival attribution with generated credit; conflating training error and interpretative error; using competition or model-size language to justify historical truth; or claiming that an archive omission is demonstrated merely because a model failed to retrieve a person. These would cross the evidential boundaries that Mackenzie's operational analysis makes visible.

# Cross-chapter and cross-lens synthesis

Chapters 1–7 successively displaced the fantasy of machine learning as an autonomous algorithm: they exposed material accumulations, diagrammatic translation, vectorised comparability, modelled objectives, probabilistic error, the production of difference and the mutual constitution of a modelled *object*. Chapter 8 asks the complementary question of **who occupies the position of knowing when such operations appear to work**. In Mackenzie's genealogy, neural-network diagrams, code and institutions of competitive evaluation redistribute the speaking authority of human and non-human learners while leaving some labour obscure (pp. 179–181, 197–208). For my theoretical framework, this strengthens **critical computational approaches** by insisting that provenance and the evidential warrant of a generated statement also have a *position*: a document attests, a model proposes, an evaluator measures, and a historian interprets, but these activities cannot silently replace one another. The strongest intersection is with **feminist and situated knowledge**, which must test whose contributions the inherited archive and the research instrument render visible, and with **critical archival theory**, which must establish the documentary standing of the traces themselves. **New working proposition:** The DDR archive can be activated more responsibly through a designed inquiry system that makes the *source, operator, evaluation and interpretation* of each proposed historical relation inspectable, including who and what remain unrepresented. **TODO (demonstrate with a source-attributed DDR case, a contested- or weak-credit case, and a production UAT result that restricts unsupported claims).**
