# Thesis job

**What the source changes for the main question:** Mackenzie describes machine-learning objects as constituted through relations among a substantive domain, its records and infrastructures, and repeatable acts of modeling, comparison and regularisation. This helps me ask what *archival object* the DDR research instrument makes available—not merely which text chunks it retrieves—and whether the resulting descriptions preserve traceable relationships to the source documents.

**Connection to the secondary question:** An instrument may assemble promising new perspectives on DDR-era design research, but modelled salience, ranking and apparent conceptual coherence do not establish which historical ideas should inform present practice. That judgement requires historical interpretation and a separately articulated contemporary design-research criterion.

**Why I am reading this now:** Chapter 3 established the consequences of vectorising material; Chapter 4 distinguished transforming functions from partial observations; Chapter 5 challenged the claim to possess 'all data'; Chapter 6 showed how distinct machine learners institute patterns of difference. Chapter 7 shows how such operations and their domain referentials **materialise particular objects of knowledge**, both stabilising them and opening new variations.

**My benchmark:** I will use this chapter if (1) I can trace a purported DDR 'object' or relation from an actual document and catalogue context through the transformations that made it available to an embedding, map or RAI answer; and (2) I can demonstrate with named, versioned cases that the instrument's rankings, omissions and model variations remain corrigible by archival evidence.

# Context, method and central argument

Mackenzie opens by asking about the *materiality* of machine learning, not simply its computation (pp. 151–152). His chosen extended case is genomic science, which appears repeatedly as an authoritative testing ground for machine-learning techniques. He adopts Foucault's concept of a **referential**: the field within which objects, relations and statements can appear and acquire authority (p. 152). DNA and genomics are not treated as a natural 'raw' dataset untouched by modelling. Genome sequences must themselves be assembled from fragments (pp. 154–155); the subsequent expression datasets can be extraordinarily wide, sparsely sampled, mixed and difficult to interpret (pp. 153, 162–163, 165–166). Genomic practice and machine learning validate, reshape and constrain one another through infrastructures, classification, feature selection and model comparison (pp. 157–161, 166–171). The large MicroArray Quality Control effort then demonstrates just how varied the predictions can be even when multiple teams examine similar biomedical tasks (pp. 171–175). The chapter closes by debating instrumental knowledge, disciplinary ordering and the ability of computational practice to generate new, not yet settled, objects of inquiry (pp. 175–177).

**Main argument:** A machine-learning 'object' is not simply a thing in the world plus a mathematical description of it. Objects and models are repeatedly constituted within entanglements of domain-specific experiments, datasets, infrastructures, institutional authority, modelling choices, comparisons and regulation. This is Mackenzie's philosophical and historical interpretation of the genomics case. It is not an empirical evaluation of archives or of the DDR model.

# Eight critical claims

## Claim 1 — The domain being studied supplies a *referential*, not just neutral input data

**Author claim:** Mackenzie draws on Foucault's referential to argue that a scientific domain does more than donate training examples; it gives a field in which machine-generated classifications become meaningful, authoritative or contestable (pp. 151–152). **Evidence-supported claim:** The chapter traces genomics from the DNA microarray example in *The Elements of Statistical Learning* through biological databases, model-building and later scientific use (pp. 151–152, 157). The referential is explicitly introduced as a condition for the emergence and delimitation of objects and relations (p. 152). **Warrant:** A computational result only acquires domain-specific significance within pre-existing scientific questions, measurements, categories and practices; the result cannot set those standards by numerical operation alone. **My inference:** The DDR archive is a *documentary and design-historical referential* for an inquiry instrument: archive catalogue descriptions, provenance, testimonies and design-research concepts are needed to interpret any apparent semantic relationship. ## Claim 2 — Even an apparently elementary 'complete' data object is already assembled and historically mutable

**Author claim:** What machine learners take to be a whole genome is materially and algorithmically constructed from many fragments, while its significance changes as researchers move from discrete disease-gene expectations to multiple forms of variation (pp. 153–156). **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie notes that sequencing machines produce fragment sets rather than a single complete genome and that computational/statistical assembly must put them in order (pp. 154–155). The discussion of pre- and post-genomic research contrasts the search for specific disease genes with a stronger interest in variation and changing gene expression (pp. 155–156). **Warrant:** Whole, apparently self-evident domain objects can depend on technical assembly and conceptual framing; one may obtain a more manipulable representation without closing the empirical question of what the object does or means. **My inference:** A 'DDR corpus' is likewise a designed and bounded research object: scanned pages, OCR, catalogue-to-PID links, documents, chunks and model representations are non-equivalent forms. Nor should an indexed corpus be equated with the total historical DDR archive. **Boundary:** Biological genome assembly is a specific statistical/algorithmic problem and cannot be used as a literal explanation of OCR, document-chunking or historical synthesis. Mackenzie explicitly distinguishes earlier probabilistic sequence assembly from tasks labelled 'machine learning' at the time (p. 155, note 4). **Consequence:** Declare corpus scope, representation levels, omissions, and transformations, and prevent downstream summaries from displacing the source record. **Practice cross-check:** Compare a page of one PID-backed archival record with its OCR, chunking and source display, including an omission or correction; **TODO (processing receipt, relevant PID and versioned text)**. ## Claim 3 — Scientific authority and computation are mutually staged through infrastructures and visual demonstrations

**Author claim:** Machine learning and genomics *cross-validate* each other's material standing through large-scale computing infrastructures, scientific applications, and repeated diagrams of biological data (pp. 157–160). **Evidence-supported claim:** **Table 7.1** places biological and biomedical disciplines prominently in a citation sample of machine-learning research (p. 157). The account explains that the demonstration foregrounded *computational scaling* rather than providing a domain-specific analysis of cancer biology (pp. 159–160). **Warrant:** The authority of a publicly persuasive computational display may derive jointly from recognisable scientific material and spectacular technical capacity; neither proves the substantive soundness of its biological propositions. **My inference:** My semantic atlas can similarly look authoritative because it is interactive, technically sophisticated and source-labelled. Its visual claims still require a clear disclosure of what a displayed relation means and a direct path back to original DDR sources. **Boundary:** The 2012 demonstration is a specific corporate presentation, not a controlled test of all genomics computation or of my interface. The domain-specific phrase **cross-validation** in Mackenzie's argument is broader than the particular statistical procedure of partitioning data into folds; these senses should not be conflated. **Consequence:** Evaluate a DDR visualisation for interpretative legibility and evidence accessibility as well as usability or aesthetics, and state when an apparent relation remains investigatory. **Practice cross-check:** Record one saved atlas state with parameters, source IDs, its caption/legend and a tested contrary reading; **TODO (screenshot, source passages, model/projection release, reviewer judgement)**. ## Claim 4 — 'Wide, dirty and mixed' objects exceed the clean pedagogical table

**Author claim:** Genomic research strains inherited statistical data forms through many features, few observations, mixed variables and noisy or missing measurements, and this very difficulty stimulates model innovation (pp. 153, 161–166). **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie describes the **p much greater than N** situation on p. 153, contrasts the small, clean iris dataset in **Table 7.2** (p. 162) with the microarray dataset of **2,308 gene features and 63 samples** (pp. 162–163), and examines its hierarchical clustering in **Figure 7.2** (p. 165). ## Claim 5 — Regularisation selects an analytically tractable *version* of the object

**Author claim:** Regularisation orders a proliferating field of predictive variables by shrinking or eliminating model coefficients in response to an optimisation criterion, effectively changing which parts of an object can matter to the model (pp. 167–171). **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie connects Foucault's disciplinary ranking to the many statistical methods applied to microarray data (pp. 167–168), develops **lasso** as a penalised regression example in **Equations 7.1–7.2** (p. 169), and shows coefficient paths in **Figure 7.3** (p. 170). On p. 171 he cites analyses in which a dataset of 16,063 measured genes yields different predictive feature subsets—1,429 genes under one approach and 384 under another. **Warrant:** Selecting features according to predictive relevance makes a new, condensed analytical object; the narrowing of attention is not a direct statement that the excluded biological material is unreal or historically unimportant. **My inference:** For DDR, the focus produced by a retrieval ranking, interface filter or chosen evidential answer packet similarly changes *what becomes available for interpretation*. I must keep the excluded or low-ranked record potentially revisit-able when asking what the corpus can establish. **Boundary:** An evidential admissibility guard in RAI is **not mathematical L1/L2 regularisation**, and retrieval ranking is not identical to lasso coefficient shrinkage; these are distinct mechanisms. Nor did any gene vanish physically when the model selected 384 predictor features. Mackenzie's disciplinary-power analogy itself needs empirical specification before being treated as an account of effects on particular people. **Consequence:** Document selection thresholds, the scope of displayed traces and the difference between 'not selected by the model' and 'not present in the archive'. **Practice cross-check:** Take a DDR question with rejected or low-ranked near traces and compare all admissible source evidence with the displayed answer and its selection rules; **TODO (frozen question, source IDs and model/guard versions)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 167–171]

## Claim 6 — Regulatory evaluation exposes disagreements among models, rather than settling the object

**Author claim:** As machine learning produces possible classifications of complex biological material, its results become objects of extensive comparative evaluation and scrutiny: regulation of modelling cannot remove model diversity (pp. 171–173). **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie introduces the US-led **MicroArray Quality Control (MAQC)** projects and their concern with technical reliability and false discovery (pp. 171–172). He reports that MAQC-II involved 36 teams and 19,779 submitted classification models, with 9,742 based on k-nearest-neighbour methods (p. 172); a specific team generated **463,320 k-NN models** by varying feature selection, distance, neighbour count, weighting and thresholds (p. 173). **Warrant:** Variation across analytical choices can remain extensive despite shared research purposes, datasets and comparison regimes. The validity and practical importance of a proposed result must be evaluated within the specific task and domain, not inferred from the method name or volume of generated results. **My inference:** DDR's production UAT is a useful record of a designed instrument's ability to retrieve and sustain bounded propositions, but shifting responses and retrieval states across releases need to be documented rather than interpreted as fresh historical discoveries. **Boundary:** MAQC is a biomedical evaluation programme, not a historical-knowledge protocol; the large counts do not imply that DDR should generate hundreds of thousands of model versions, and 'cross-validation' at this point includes domain-level public scrutiny as well as formal statistical practices. **Consequence:** Preserve the model/retriever/corpus and evaluation-version identity for each answer; report pass, refusal and bounded insufficiency as different research outcomes. **Practice cross-check:** Compare one unchanged DDR test question over identified production releases using the same evidence scope, with human PASS/FAIL and documented disagreements; **TODO (UAT IDs, release SHAs and evidence packet)**. ## Claim 7 — Neighbourhoods are conditional constructs and can be strikingly unstable

**Author claim:** Mackenzie uses k-nearest-neighbours to show that spatial proximity is a deliberate modelling assumption; changing k or the space's high-dimensional conditions may change decision boundaries substantially (pp. 172–175). **Evidence-supported claim:** **Figure 7.4** (p. 172) reproduces Fix and Hodges's early nearest-neighbour formulation, and the text explains how k is the chosen size of a local neighbourhood (pp. 173–174). **Figure 7.5**, p. 175, compares two neighbourhood decisions whose irregular boundaries differ, while the discussion notes high-dimensional sparsity and instability (p. 174). **Warrant:** A local grouping is meaningful *relative to a metric, representation, sample and parameter*, not merely because the points are near one another in a display. **My inference:** DDR 'semantic neighbourhood' ought to be presented as an exploratory operation, linked to the embeddings and retrieval procedure that construct it, with room for instability and source-level disagreement. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's **k-NN supervised classifier** is not UMAP's neighbourhood graph or the DDR instrument's vector retriever, even when the vocabulary overlaps; the existence and exact meaning of a k-slider in the DDR atlas requires checking the current implementation. The OCR of the p. 175 plot caption inconsistently renders one k-value, so the note does not rely on that exact number. **Consequence:** Capture k/metric/model/index settings appropriate to each actual DDR view, and do not infer influence, collaboration or testimony from visible proximity. **Practice cross-check:** Hold archival source passages fixed while changing only a documented neighbourhood control; compare which historical interpretations survive direct source reading; **TODO (view, settings, source PIDs, test result)**. ## Claim 8 — Materialising a research object is a historically and politically situated practice

**Author claim:** Mackenzie both entertains and qualifies Isabelle Stengers's concern that fast instrumental knowledge may follow established interests, proposing that machine-learning operations also multiply and transform the relations through which the biological object becomes knowable (pp. 175–177). **Evidence-supported claim:** The concluding pages describe the traffic among scientists, models, databases and biological objects (p. 176) and argue that operations of selecting and ranking may both constrain diversity and generate new statements, subject positions and forms of inquiry (pp. 176–177). The ending returns to the Foucauldian materiality of practices that order, redistribute and regulate knowledge (p. 177). **Warrant:** It is inadequate either to imagine computation passively reflecting settled reality or to presume that it can only reproduce established interests. The object and the methods used to know it evolve in a mutually consequential, contested process. **My inference:** A DDR digital research instrument could create productive occasions for revisiting neglected design ideas while also repeating cataloguing hierarchies or privileging records whose words are computationally prominent. Its value has to be argued from actual documented changes in what can be questioned, not from a promise of neutral discovery. **Boundary:** The genomic examples establish neither historical suppression nor contemporary revitalisation of DDR scholarship. Similarly, the analogy to disciplinary power and materialisation is an interpretative lens, not direct empirical proof of archival politics. **Consequence:** My reflective account should capture both successful new questions and the failure/absence/visibility patterns through which the designed instrument limits or redirects inquiry. **Practice cross-check:** Compare one case where interaction changed the research question with one where the model amplified an inadequate archival description; **TODO (dated journal entries, archive references and observed UAT outcome)**. # Terms and definitions

- **Referential (Mackenzie via Foucault):** The field or condition within which an object and its relations may emerge, be differentiated and acquire epistemic authority (p. 152). **My use:** An analogy for the historically specific DDR archive and the scholarly work through which retrieved traces earn interpretations; not a replacement for an archivist's formal definition of provenance.
- **Hyperobject:** Mackenzie's description of very large, distributed and multitemporal domains such as genomes and markets (pp. 151–152). **My use:** Cautiously, if at all: I should not call DDR an 'archival hyperobject' solely because it contains many documents.
- **Materiality / transcription and reinscription:** Foucault's emphasis, taken up by Mackenzie, on institutional possibilities of reuse, reworking and circulation rather than only the physical location of one object (p. 158). **My use:** Study how scanned records, metadata, chunks, diagrammatic interfaces and answers circulate as different artefacts; this must coexist with ordinary archival materiality.
- **p≫N:** Many measured features relative to the number of observed samples, prominent in genomic data (p. 153). **My use:** A specific statistical dimension problem, *not* shorthand for 'a big archive' or 'many chunks'.
- **Cross-validation (two uses):** Mackenzie's broader mutual reinforcement of scientific domain and machine-learning authority (pp. 157–161, 166–167); formal statistical/evaluation procedures belong to a distinct technical meaning. **My use:** Specify which meaning is intended and do not imply a successful cross-validation study occurred in DDR unless one is documented.
- **Wide, dirty, mixed:** Many features, absent/noisy entries, and numerical/categorical combinations, used in genomic modelling (pp. 162–163). **My use:** An analogy for why tidy showcase datasets differ from historical archival heterogeneity, without presuming identical statistics.
- **Regularisation / lasso:** Penalised modelling that shrinks or selects coefficients to constrain prediction (pp. 167–171). **My use:** Critically examine **selection** in the DDR instrument but never rename provenance guarding or retrieval filtering 'lasso regularisation'.
- **k-nearest-neighbours:** A neighbourhood-based predictive technique whose outcomes depend on the representation, distance and selected k (pp. 172–175). **My use:** Motivate explicit disclosure of relevant semantic-neighbourhood settings, while separately citing sources describing UMAP or the actual retriever.

# My critical response

This is one of the richest chapters for **critical design practice**, because the *presentation* of the analytical object is itself part of Mackenzie's evidence. **Table 7.1** (p. 157) provides a quantitative picture of the disciplinary distribution of machine-learning literature; **Figure 7.1** (p. 159) makes a Circos diagram of the human genome seem like a navigable and spectacular object, while the adjacent account reveals that a software conference foregrounded compute scale rather than biomedical explanation (pp. 158–160). **Table 7.2** (p. 162) shows the spare and complete iris rows against which the wider genomic case is developed, and **Figure 7.2** (p. 165) shows two hierarchical clustering views of gene-expression data—readable *patterns* that presuppose extensive unseen measurement and annotation. **Figure 7.3** (p. 170) displays how lasso coefficients appear and fade under constraint; **Figure 7.4** (p. 172) exposes the historical and procedural roots of nearest-neighbour classification; and **Figure 7.5** (p. 175) makes the instability of a constructed boundary visually apparent. Their cumulative message for a designer is that diagrams can make new analytical objects available for critical reading, but they cannot silently import their operational authority into the source domain.

My reservations are source-specific. **First**, Foucault's referential/materiality concepts (pp. 152, 158) are theoretically suggestive, but the movement from scientific reuse to power and institutional authority is not equally demonstrated for every example; a strong thesis claim needs documentation of what was selected, by whom and with what effects. **Second**, the Google example (pp. 158–160) can show the rhetorical and infrastructural staging of machine learning, but cannot establish that the showcased genomic associations were scientifically useful. **Third**, the powerful reduction of gene predictors on p. 171 describes selected *analytic features*, not physical reduction of the biological genome—rhetorically calling the subset a changed genome risks obscuring the difference. **Fourth**, model proliferation in MAQC-II (pp. 171–173) is strong evidence for methodological contingency, but large numbers of configurations are not themselves proof of unreliability; careful validation can select useful models. **Fifth**, p. 164 is definitely absent from this scan. The source discussion of **Table 7.3** is therefore incomplete and should be revisited after rescanning. Also, p. 178's possible presence or function remains to be checked.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** *The DDR research instrument does not simply represent an existing archive in a new medium; through selection, numerical transformation, ranking and interface presentation, it constitutes particular objects of inquiry whose relations to the underlying testamentary traces must remain inspectable, revisable and historically accountable.*

# Cross-chapter and cross-lens synthesis

Chapters 1–6 established a progression from heterogeneous data practice and diagrammatic operation to vectorisation, function fitting, probabilisation and the active institution of patterns. **Chapter 7 now sharpens the object of critique: the thing we believe a machine is describing can itself change in the movement among sources, datasets, fitted parameters, evaluated predictions and public representations** (pp. 151–152, 167–171, 175–177). The genomic case is compelling partly because it is not wholly neutral: laboratories, public data infrastructures, scientific authority, large compute platforms and alternative ways of selecting features shape which versions of a biological problem can be seen and acted upon. For the DDR thesis, this changes 'activation' from a promise to discover hidden facts into a more careful, practice-led proposition: the designed research instrument may create **new, revisitable objects of historical inquiry**, provided the source's documental status and the transformation that made it visible remain accountable.
