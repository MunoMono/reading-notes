# Thesis job

**Contribution to the primary question:** Mackenzie makes the statistical and probabilistic operations through which machine learners handle an apparently exhaustive dataset the object of critique. For DDR, this helps distinguish a corpus's bounded completeness, the measurements used to assess retrieval, and the independent historical question of what its records can support.

**Secondary-question relevance:** Computational comparisons may suggest DDR-period ideas worth revisiting, but probabilistic modelling, classification accuracy and corpus size do not settle their historical significance or contemporary usefulness. Their interpretation requires documentary and design-historical analysis.

**Why read this chapter now:** Chapters 1–4 established machine learning as operational practice, followed diagrammatic translations, interrogated vectorisation and examined the difference between modelling functions and their partial observers. Chapter 5 asks how *probability*, including error, model selection, classification and distributions, comes to inhabit those operations. Its historical discussion of document indexing is particularly germane to an archive-centred PhD.

**My benchmark:** (1) I will not treat 'all data', a score, ranking or classification as an unqualified historical warrant: each must state its relevant population, target, assumptions and coverage. (2) I will distinguish measurable model error from documentary incompleteness, contested attribution and historical uncertainty, and test both where possible using PID-backed source traces and human adjudication.

# Position, method and main argument

The chapter begins with Ian Hacking's *The Taming of Chance* and Peirce's deliberate introduction of chance into experimental control (p. 103). Against claims that sufficiently extensive data eliminate the problem of sampling, Mackenzie introduces the deliberately provocative **N = ∀X** as shorthand for having *all* observations of a domain, rather than merely a sample (pp. 104–105). He argues that statistical ideas have not disappeared into massive datasets; instead, they have been internalised in machine-learning apparatuses themselves—a process he calls **probabilization** (pp. 105–107). He examines statistical oppositions (**Table 5.1**, p. 107), probability distributions (pp. 108–110), Naive Bayes and conditional probabilities (pp. 111–113, 115–118), the historical connection between probabilistic indexing and documentary corpora (pp. 118–120), and the bias–variance account of model error (pp. 120–122). His conclusion returns to a population not just of measured events or individuals but of *machine-learning models whose variation can be sampled, compared and monitored* (pp. 122–124).

**The central argument:** A declaration that a system 'has all the data' does not bypass probability, selection or uncertainty. Machine learning reconfigures those statistical practices inside algorithms, model families and evaluation regimes. This is a conceptual and historical account, not an evaluation of the DDR archive, UMAP, a contemporary multilingual embedding model or a retrieval-augmented language model.

## Claim 2 — Probabilization moves statistical ordering into the operation and evaluation of machines

**Author claim:** Drawing on Hacking, Mackenzie describes a historical movement from measurement error toward the probability distributions attributed to populations, and then an inversion by which machine-learning *devices themselves* become objects of probabilistic observation (pp. 103, 106). **Evidence-supported claim:** Rather than merely applying inherited population statistics to new data, he argues that learners are compared through errors, bias, variance and probabilistic model behaviour (pp. 106–107, 123–124). **Warrant:** The relevant object of statistical management shifts when a model's predictions and errors are routinely monitored as part of the apparatus that produces knowledge. **My inference:** DDR model evaluations, benchmark questions and comparative release results are evidence *about instrument performance*; they should not be mistaken for measurements of DDR history itself. **Boundary:** 'Statistics inside out' is Mackenzie's interpretative historical analogy, not a demonstrated one-to-one reversal across every field or every machine-learning approach. **Consequence:** Explicitly locate what UAT observes—retrieval, source admissibility, response quality—and what it leaves undecided about past events. **Practice cross-check:** Inspect a versioned DDR UAT round and a corresponding original document; **TODO (record model release, test suite and assessed question)**. ## Claim 3 — The discipline's familiar oppositions shape what practitioners see as a learning problem

## Claim 5 — Naive Bayes illustrates useful classification built on drastic simplification

## Claim 7 — Document retrieval has its own historical 'ancestral' practices that machine learning inherits

## Claim 8 — Bias, variance and generalisation error mark a limit that 'all the data' cannot remove

**Author claim:** Mackenzie explains the bias–variance decomposition as a way to make a model's sources of prediction error intelligible and to show why simplicity may outperform greater apparent fidelity. **Evidence-supported claim:** He defines variance in relation to changing training samples and bias in relation to simplified model form (p. 120); p. 121 discusses their trade-off, the difference between training and test error, and the possibility that more complex models fit their training material more closely while generalising less well. He returns to error estimation and resampling strategies on p. 122. **Warrant:** Seeing many or even all cases in one selected dataset does not establish that a fitted structure will generalise to new data or answer a different question successfully. **My inference:** DDR production UAT should use independent historical questions, adversarial near matches and scoped missingness, rather than only the questions used while tuning the instrument. Retrieval quality and claim-evidence integrity should be judged separately and against held-out cases where feasible. **Boundary:** *Statistical bias* in the bias–variance decomposition is **not identical** to institutional, gendered or historiographical bias, although the concepts can prompt different lines of inquiry. The chapter's technical account does not itself validate the design or independence of current DDR UAT. **Consequence:** State which test cases were used during model iteration and which were independently assessed, and resist presenting an in-sample improvement as general validity. **Practice cross-check:** Compare revised and held-out DDR UAT question results against the frozen rubric; **TODO (identify suite versions and independence constraints)**. ## Claim 9 — Models themselves become a population, and epistemic uncertainty moves into evaluation

# Terms and definitions for the thesis

- **N = ∀X (Mackenzie's critical expression):** A shorthand for the aspiration to have every observation in a chosen dataset or domain, rather than a sample (p. 104). **My use:** A challenge to declare the **scope** of any assertion of corpus completeness. The typeset logical symbol is verified from the page image; OCR often renders it as V.
- **Probabilization:** Mackenzie's term for the way probabilistic distributions and statistical forms increasingly configure machine learners as devices and populations of models (pp. 105–107, 122–124). **My use:** An operational lens on uncertainty measures and model comparisons, not a label for every ambiguous archival document.
- **Probability distribution:** A mathematical relation assigning probabilities or probability densities to possible values/outcomes and their variations (pp. 108–110). **My use:** Only where a technical process actually employs such a formal quantity; neither a raw embedding distance nor a generative answer is automatically a calibrated probability.
- **Conditional probability:** The probability of an event or classification given another observed event or feature, exemplified by Naive Bayes (pp. 111–112). **My use:** To examine models' assumptions and input framing; *not* a substitute for documentary corroboration.
- **Naive Bayes:** A probabilistic classification family represented in the chapter by multiplying class-conditional feature densities under a simplifying conditional-independence assumption (pp. 111–112). **My use:** A historical demonstration that operational success can coexist with simplified document representation—not a description of DDR's embedding model.
- **Generative/discriminative and prediction/inference:** Statistical distinctions Mackenzie identifies as organising machine-learning literature (**Table 5.1**, p. 107). **My use:** Terms that require explicit definition before being deployed in a mixed archival/RAI methodology.
- **Bias–variance decomposition:** A way of assessing aspects of model prediction error associated with simplifying assumptions and changes of fitted model under different training datasets (pp. 120–122). **My use:** A reason to inspect generalisation and compare cases across versions; separate from the critique of social or archival bias.
- **Ancestral probabilization:** Earlier systems of classificatory practice and probabilistic understanding that contemporary models inherit as pre-organised tasks and categories (pp. 118–120). **My use:** A question about the catalogue and document-retrieval traditions already framing DDR's digitised evidence.

# My critical response

The chapter's most relevant achievement is its refusal to let 'all the data' become a guarantee of objectivity. The conceptual **N = ∀X** (p. 104) performs useful diagnostic work: any apparently complete dataset is complete only in relation to a defined reference domain and a set of operational decisions. The genealogical argument is strongest where it becomes materially specific. **Table 5.1 (p. 107)** names practical statistical distinctions; **Equation 5.2 (p. 108)** ties probability to population description and control; **Equation 5.3 (p. 111)** displays a strikingly economical classification principle; and the discussion of Maron's early document indexing (pp. 119–120) shows how existing subject categories and term selection prepare the apparently 'automatic' production of document relevance. The bias–variance discussion (pp. 120–122) gives a clear account of why successful fitted behaviour is not simply a matter of accumulating more examples.

I would nonetheless qualify four moves. **First**, the broad claim that machine learning 'reverses' statistical history (pp. 106, 123–124) is a provocative interpretative genealogy rather than an empirical test establishing one universal historical reversal. **Second**, the distinction between the probability of a class and the utility of a classifier is sometimes rhetorically compressed: Naive Bayes can classify adequately without its raw probability estimates necessarily being well calibrated (pp. 115–117, 122), a distinction to retain carefully. **Third**, the phrase 'all the data' conflates at least three different referents unless made explicit: all digitised records, all included model inputs and all historical traces of the domain (pp. 104–105, 123). **Fourth**, while the genealogical narrative convincingly connects probabilistic indexing and text classification (pp. 118–120), it cannot by itself explain the mechanisms or ethical consequences of any particular contemporary learned representation.

# Cross-chapter and cross-lens synthesis
