# Thesis job

**How this advances the primary research question:** Mackenzie asks what it means for machines to 'learn' by finding and adjusting mathematical functions, particularly when the *functions that transform data* are coupled to *functions that measure their effects*. This allows me to distinguish a computational procedure's internally defined success from the historical warrant of a proposition about DDR people, projects or ideas.

**Secondary-question relevance:** Machine-learning outputs may help identify connections worth reconsidering in present-day design research, but a loss value, classification probability or visually clear boundary does not judge whether a DDR-period idea is historically significant or presently useful. This further judgement belongs to documentary analysis and critical design historiography.

**Why read it now:** Chapters 1–3 established an archaeology of machine-learning operations, diagrammatic transformations and vector-space comparability. Chapter 4 specifies how a fitted function is sought and assessed: it turns attention from what a representation makes comparable to **which objective a system is actually organised to improve**. This is crucial for a practice-led DDR instrument incorporating embedding-based retrieval, semantic visualisation and RAI answer synthesis.

**My benchmark:** (1) Every reported computational 'success' must name its task, measure, available reference and scope; it must not silently become a claim of historical truth. (2) Any proposed DDR relationship must be checked against identifiable contemporary or retrospective evidence and receive an explicit interpretation—supported, ambiguous or insufficient—independent of numerical rank, optimisation or fluency.

# Position and argument

Mackenzie opens with a tension between the aspiration to a single universal learner and the apparently limitless variety of methods and implementations (pp. 75–77). He argues that a relative commonality lies in functions that map inputs to outputs, and in the observation and iterative adjustment of those functions (pp. 77–83). With Isabelle Stengers he then asks whether a function can genuinely 'learn' outside the experimental relations that give scientific claims their reference (pp. 83–85). The book's extended worked example couples linear models to the logistic/sigmoid function (pp. 86–92), compares model variants in regularisation and decision diagrams (pp. 93–95), and examines cost functions and gradient-based optimisation (pp. 95–100). The closing pages argue that all such observers remain partial, even as the assemblage produces novel classifications and possibilities for thought (pp. 100–102).

**Scope:** This chapter primarily addresses statistical modelling, classifiers, logistic regression, optimisation and mathematical/diagrammatic accounts of machine learning as understood in 2017. It does **not** examine the DDR archive, bge-m3 text embeddings, UMAP or Qwen-based retrieval-augmented inference. Connections to those elements below are explicitly researcher-side proposals requiring DDR practice evidence.

# The author's main move

Mackenzie reverses the tempting idea that 'learning' names a free-standing intelligence possessed by a model. In his account, a **function changes data**, a second **function evaluates an aspect of the resulting changes**, and an **optimisation procedure uses that partial observation** to search across possible parameter settings. Human researchers, code libraries, visualisations and experimental contexts participate in that arrangement. Its power lies in the relays between functions, not in any isolated function's ability to know the world (especially pp. 84–86, 95–102).

## Claim 2 — Learning is posed as finding a *useful approximation*, not recovering an intrinsic truth

**Author claim:** The mathematical function connects inputs and outputs, while function finding estimates a relationship from empirical data, often with many admissible approximations. **Evidence-supported claim:** The chapter defines functions in the mathematical context and says that no unique function perfectly represents a given dataset (p. 80); it describes classifiers as functions and cites the distinction between an assumed data-generating relation and an estimated approximation (pp. 81–82). **Warrant:** A model's selection is conditional on a representational task, a hypothesis space and a measure of what counts as adequate. ## Claim 3 — Evaluation is governed by what can be observed, and unsupervised methods expose an evidential problem

**Author claim:** Mackenzie distinguishes supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning through forms of observability, drawing particular attention to the lack of a direct known-target success measure in unsupervised learning. **Evidence-supported claim:** He quotes *The Elements of Statistical Learning* on expected loss and cross-validation in supervised tasks versus absent direct ground-truth success criteria for an unsupervised pattern (p. 81), and treats partial observation as a productive source of method variation (pp. 80–81). **Warrant:** A convincing cluster or partition does not come with the same kind of check as an explicitly labelled test case, so a criterion of 'good' grouping needs to be specified separately. **My inference:** UMAP neighbourhoods or semantic atlas groupings should not be adjudicated solely by visual coherence; research questions, source checks and interpretative comparison must evaluate whether they are useful. **Boundary:** The quoted contrast does not mean unsupervised techniques can *never* be assessed; stability tests, task-specific downstream checks and expert judgments can be devised, but none establishes a universal historical ground truth. Furthermore, bge-m3 embeddings and UMAP involve different learning objectives and training histories, which this chapter does not analyse. **Consequence:** Explicitly separate map-quality and retrieval adequacy from historically supported conclusions. **Practice cross-check:** Review a DDR semantic atlas neighbourhood against its source texts and a documented expert assessment, and capture parameters; **TODO (atlas state and source IDs)**. ## Claim 4 — Operational and observational functions are distinct, interdependent roles

**Author claim:** A function in machine learning may *act* on data, while another function may *observe* or measure a consequence of that action. This double arrangement is often neglected in accounts that say simply that the machine 'learns'. **Evidence-supported claim:** The chapter identifies a function-fitting model alongside a measure of approximation (pp. 82–83), then states that an operational function transforms vector space and an observational function generates information about fit, success and error (p. 85). Graphs may portray variations in the model rather than showing underlying experimental data (pp. 85–86). **Warrant:** The authority of an observed value depends on which action was evaluated, on what data, against which target—not just on the presence of a quantitative result. **My inference:** My practice should visibly distinguish the operations that propose source passages or synthesise text from the separate operations that check the evidential validity of claims. In particular, **retrieval, generation and human UAT adjudication are different acts**; their outputs must not be conflated. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's mathematical notion of operational/observational functions cannot be mapped one-for-one onto the design of an RAI evidence guard, which may use rules, human review or other kinds of checks. Nor does a low training loss itself validate generated documentary assertions. **Consequence:** Provide an operation-by-operation account of what the DDR validation layer can and cannot verify. **Practice cross-check:** Trace a production RAI question through retrieval, source attribution, answer draft, guard decision and final human judgement; **TODO (production evidence packet and version)**. ## Claim 6 — A sigmoid's shape turns continuous scores into usable class probabilities, but classes still require framing

## Claim 7 — Model diagrams show *variations in fitting*, not simply facts about observed cases

## Claim 8 — The loss function defines what improvement means, and optimisation pursues *that* objective

**Author claim:** Much of what is called learning involves repeated selection of parameters to optimise an explicit cost, loss or objective function rather than solving a relationship once and for all. **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie contrasts closed-form estimation with iterative search (pp. 92, 95), names optimisation techniques on p. 95, and explains the relation between loss, known outcomes and parameter variation on p. 96. Equation 4.3 presents a log-likelihood objective whose terms guide a search rather than yielding a simple closed-form solution (p. 97). **Warrant:** A system can correctly improve its stated numerical objective without improving another value the researcher ultimately cares about. A higher retrieval score must not override source-level rejection; strong UAT should privilege admissibility over a merely polished synthesis. **Boundary:** The 'loss' in model training and the evaluative dispositions in production UAT are not the same mathematical quantity; the latter may be human or rules-based. ## Claim 9 — Gradient methods turn rates of change into partial observers, leaving reference unresolved

**Author claim:** Iterative gradient optimisation can 'observe' how an objective changes as model parameters change; it never sees everything that matters about the world's relation to the model. **Evidence-supported claim:** The chapter gives a gradient-based update and handwritten derivation (**Equation 4.4 / Figure 4.5**, pp. 97–98) and a stylised descent path (**Figure 4.6**, p. 99); it explains how derivatives indicate a direction of reduced cost (pp. 99–100). The conclusion explicitly calls people and machines *partial observers*: models transform data, optimisers see selected errors, and humans cannot directly view full multidimensional relationships (pp. 100–101). On p. 102 Mackenzie considers whether limited observation in situated practice might still allow unexpected differences and inventive moves. **Warrant:** Improved fit demonstrates a constrained kind of learning relative to a defined objective; it does not create complete visibility into how the source domain works. **My inference:** The DDR instrument's epistemic contribution may be strongest when it makes its own partiality observable: what was retrieved, what was missing, why a synthetic claim was declined, and what additional source inspection would be needed. **Boundary:** Gradient descent is discussed as part of fitting a statistical classifier; it cannot be transferred literally to the inference-time process of answering a DDR archival query. The 'partial observer' argument is a philosophical analysis, not a quantified prediction of retrieval failure. **Consequence:** Include both successful and refused/limited UAT responses in the dissertation's evaluation, using production evidence and corpus boundaries. **Practice cross-check:** Compare source-backed PASS, unsupported-claim refusal and scoped-missingness cases across a single fixed UAT release; **TODO (exact case IDs, dates, versions and review verdicts)**. # Definitions and distinctions worth retaining

- **Function finding:** Estimating a useful relationship between inputs and outputs from data rather than deriving a uniquely true description of its source phenomenon (pp. 80–82). **My use:** A conceptual warning against reading computational predictions as documentary facts.
- **Operational function:** In Mackenzie's analysis, a function that transforms modelled data (p. 85). **My use:** A way to ask which DDR pipeline components change the source's representation or produce a candidate answer, *not* a label for every software function.
- **Observational function:** A function that represents fit, error or the consequences of a model's transformations (pp. 85–86). **My use:** A way to ask which measurement or rule warrants a reported status and what it can observe.
- **Loss / cost / objective function:** A chosen measurable criterion used to guide model fitting by maximisation or minimisation (pp. 95–97). **My use:** A reason to identify success criteria separately for semantic relevance, source admissibility and historical interpretation; not to claim the UAT rubric *is* a neural-network loss.
- **Gradient:** The rates of change of an objective with respect to its parameters, used to identify directions for iterative improvement (pp. 97–100). **My use:** A reminder of bounded procedural observation within learning; not evidence that historical provenance can be differentiated mathematically.
- **Supervised vs unsupervised evaluation:** The difference, in Mackenzie's treatment, between a task with known desired targets and pattern discovery without the same direct check (pp. 80–81). **My use:** Distinguish technical evaluation of groupings from expert historical assessment.
- **Partial observer:** A human, function or optimisation procedure that can apprehend certain transformations and errors without complete access to the relations it purports to describe (pp. 100–101). **My use:** A framework for documenting the DDR apparatus's epistemic scope and declared missingness.
- **Experimental relay / reference (through Stengers):** A situated connection among functions, things, instruments and observers through which a formal result earns substantive reference (pp. 83–85). **My use:** An analogy for the source-verification steps required between retrieved passage and historical proposition.

# My critical assessment

Mackenzie's distinction between *transforming* and *observing* functions is particularly productive because it dismantles the fiction that 'learning' is one indivisible achievement. **Figure 4.1** (p. 76) shows the practitioner's map of methods; **Figure 4.2** (p. 87) shows how parameter variation changes the logistic curve; **Figures 4.3–4.4** (pp. 93–94) show different types of model-related visual knowledge; and **Figures 4.5–4.6** (pp. 98–99) show an iterative optimisation procedure becoming something that can be diagrammed and followed. Together they make visible why a procedure's notion of 'working' is constrained by the observational conventions built into it.

The chapter deserves qualified rather than reverential adoption. Its suggestion that machine learning is united by function finding is analytically fruitful, but it is not a comprehensive technical classification of every algorithmic or generative model, let alone models released after 2017 (pp. 80–82). There is an imprecision in the account of mathematical functions on p. 80 ('one-to-one'), and the logistic explanation on pp. 88 and 91 can read as though smooth probability output were already the same thing as a binary class decision, whereas the threshold matters. The relatively sharp division between supervised and unsupervised 'success' (p. 81) is illuminating but should not be interpreted as barring all evaluation of unsupervised structure. More conceptually, Stengers's experimental reference is strongly persuasive as a limit on computational overclaiming (pp. 83–85), yet a chain of empirical archival corroboration is not simply equivalent to a laboratory's experiment. I must demonstrate the validity of that analogy within archival scholarship and my own evidence.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** *I distinguish computational operations that generate or transform candidate archival relationships from the procedures by which those relationships acquire evidential warrant: improving a model's objective may increase analytical usefulness, but historical knowledge still depends on attributable traces, contextual interpretation and explicit limits.*

# Boundary and risk

# Cross-chapter and cross-lens synthesis

Chapters 1–3 progressively locate machine learning as a heterogeneous material practice, identify diagrammatic translations among text/code/figures, and show how vectorisation makes otherwise dissimilar material computationally comparable. Chapter 4 now makes the status of a *successful result* itself questionable: functions are sought and transformed, other functions observe a chosen kind of error, and optimisation systematically improves that partial measure (pp. 80–85, 95–101). This sharpens the **critical computational approaches** lens at a point where the dissertation must distinguish an engaging pattern or plausible synthetic answer from a source-supported claim about contested design knowledge. Critical archival theory still has to supply an account of records, description, provenance and loss; critical design historiography must judge the relationships and disagreements; and feminist/situated knowledge must scrutinise who is rendered visible by both archived and computational classifications. The resulting **working thesis proposition** is that a designed DDR inquiry system can activate testamentary traces most responsibly when it makes *its own criteria of relevance and evidential judgement* inspectable and capable of failure.
