# Position + moment (2–4 lines)

Zaagsma writes from digital history at the Luxembourg Centre for Contemporary and Digital History and situates the paper within the critical turn in digital humanities: tool criticism, data criticism, algorithmic criticism, interface criticism and digital source criticism. His intervention is synthetic rather than empirical, drawing across history, archival science, library studies and critical heritage scholarship to construct a model of the politics of digitisation.

He explicitly acknowledges that his own literature base is linguistically skewed towards English, German and Dutch and that the archival model he adapts has Western origins, making the article partly reflexive about the uneven knowledge structures it critiques.

**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):**  
The paper predates widespread archival use of generative AI, but its model becomes more urgent when retrieval and synthesis can conceal earlier selection processes behind fluent answers. Computational accessibility should therefore be treated as another mediated layer of the archive rather than as an escape from archival politics.

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)

They try to make the constitution of the digital historical record critically legible by modelling digitisation as a sequence of political decisions about control, selection, description and access that shape which histories can subsequently be researched.

# Three-claim evidence ledger (max 3 claims)

## Claim 1

- **Claim (plain):** Digitised archives are selections of an already selected historical record and should not be mistaken for complete evidential collections.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Zaagsma argues that increased access to digitised sources does not imply completeness and that many materials are not, and never will be, digitised. Digitisation therefore entails selecting from collections that are themselves the product of prior archival selection. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 830–831, 836]` He later describes retro-digitised archives explicitly as “re-selections” of the human record. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]`
- **Warrant (my words):** A computational corpus inherits at least two boundaries before retrieval begins: what survived or was collected and what was subsequently chosen for digitisation. Absence from the digital corpus therefore cannot establish absence from the historical archive.
- **So what for my thesis (a reusable sentence):** The DDR digital corpus is an evidence surface produced through successive selections rather than a complete representation of the archival record, so computational missingness must remain scoped to that defined surface.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin Absences / scoped missingness: when a query returns no evidence, distinguish “not present in the indexed corpus” from “not present in the DDR archive” and from the stronger historical claim that the event, actor or relation did not exist.

## Claim 2

- **Claim (plain):** Metadata, classification, OCR, search and interface design actively determine which digitised materials become intellectually visible to researchers.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Zaagsma argues that classification and metadata reproduce or reconfigure earlier structures of knowledge and that metadata directly determine findability; poor metadata can prevent relevant material from being located. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 841–842]` He treats interface design and search as new mediators of archival access and warns that keyword-driven “surgical discovery” can bypass collection-level context and reduce opportunities for browsing and serendipitous discovery. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 842–844]`
- **Warrant (my words):** Digitisation does not simply expose historical material. The subsequent descriptive and retrieval infrastructure determines what can be found, how records are grouped and which relationships appear salient.
- **So what for my thesis:** Computational visibility is not equivalent to archival presence: a DDR trace can be digitally present while remaining functionally absent because its metadata, transcription, embedding or interface position prevents it from surfacing during inquiry.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin Semantic Atlas / Research Query: compare search, semantic neighbourhood and metadata pathways for the same DDR actors and concepts and record cases where one mode surfaces material that another effectively hides.

## Claim 3

- **Claim (plain):** Responsible digital historical research requires transparency about what the digital resource contains, how it was constituted and what relevant sources remain outside it.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** In his recommendations, Zaagsma calls for “digital cultural heritage transparency guidelines” documenting funding, selection criteria, metadata and classification, access mechanisms, interface/search decisions and relevant offline sources. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 844]` He stresses that cataloguing is a fundamental precondition for access and concludes that not knowing what sources exist introduces “fundamental epistemological biases” into historical research. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 844–845]`
- **Warrant (my words):** Researchers cannot assess evidential adequacy if the boundaries and construction of the digital corpus remain invisible. Provenance must therefore extend beyond individual documents to the conditions under which the searchable corpus itself was constituted.
- **So what for my thesis:** Evidential provenance for retrieval-augmented historical research should include corpus provenance: what has been digitised and indexed, what remains outside the computational evidence surface and which technical transformations mediate retrieval.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin methodology: document the corpus boundary, source types, digitisation/transcription status and indexing process alongside passage-level citations, and state explicitly that scoped missingness applies to that evidence surface rather than the DDR historical universe.

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)

- **Digital selectivity:** awareness that digitised resources represent selections rather than complete documentary records and that their composition shapes historical research. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]`
- **Explicit selection:** intentional decisions concerning which materials are included or excluded from digitisation. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]`
- **Implicit selection:** the way users’ engagement with digitised materials is shaped by the search, retrieval and interface options available to them. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]`
- **Surgical discovery:** targeted retrieval that moves directly to individual items or passages and can bypass the meso- and macro-level contexts encountered through physical archival browsing. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 843–844]`
- **Digital archival literacy:** the capacity to understand and critically evaluate how digitised archival resources have been selected, structured, described and made accessible. `[@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 844–845]`
- **Digital evidence surface:** my term for the subset of archival evidence made computationally available through digitisation, transcription, description and indexing.
- **Corpus provenance:** my extension of provenance from individual records to the construction and limits of the digital evidence surface itself.

# My response (no antithesis; state positives)

- **What I take from this (1–3 bullets):**
  - This provides a foundational digital-history argument for scoped missingness: absence inside a computational corpus cannot automatically be projected back onto the historical record.
  - The distinction between explicit and implicit selection is extremely useful. Selection occurs both before digitisation and again through metadata, retrieval and interface design.
  - Zaagsma's insistence on exposing relevant offline material expands provenance from “where did this record come from?” to “what evidential universe was available to the system in the first place?”

- **What I reframe / adjust (1–2 bullets, stated positively):**
  - I carry his digitisation model one stage further into retrieval-augmented inference: digitisation → description/OCR → indexing/embedding → retrieval → inferential synthesis each creates another potential boundary between archival presence and computational visibility.
  - I treat interface context as methodologically important rather than decorative: semantic neighbourhoods and comparative views can restore some of the relational context that single-result search and generated answers tend to flatten.

- **What question it raises next (1–2 bullets):**
  - How should Turin expose the boundary between indexed DDR material and relevant analogue or unindexed material so that users do not interpret computational silence as archival silence?
  - Can the Semantic Atlas recover some of the browsing, context and serendipity lost through query-driven “surgical discovery”?

# Related works (only if it directly connects)

- Moss, Thomas and Gollins (2018), *The Reconfiguration of the Archive as Data to Be Mined* — examines the epistemological consequences of transforming archival collections into computational datasets.
