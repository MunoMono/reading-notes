# Thesis job

**How this source moves the primary research question forward:** Zaagsma shows that the digital historical record is produced through successive political and technical selections rather than simply reproduced online. This gives the thesis a rigorous basis for scoped missingness and for treating digitisation, metadata, OCR, search and interface design as part of the evidential conditions of computational archival research.

**How this source bears on the secondary question:** Revisiting DDR-period ideas depends on which traces survive into the computational evidence surface and how they are described, indexed and retrieved. Digital access can open new historical questions while also reproducing older archival canons and inequalities.

**Why I’m reading this now:** It provides the clearest digital-history account of how digitised evidence is constituted before retrieval or generative inference begins.

**Where it sits in my argument:** Contemporary bridge literature supporting corpus provenance, scoped missingness and critical interface design.

**My benchmark for using it:** I will use Zaagsma where I need to distinguish digital availability from archival completeness and where I need to identify the specific stage at which evidence becomes inaccessible or invisible.

# Position + moment

Zaagsma writes from digital history within the “critical turn” in digital humanities, drawing together tool criticism, data criticism, algorithmic criticism, interface criticism and digital source criticism. The article is synthetic rather than experimental: it constructs a framework for analysing the politics of digitisation across selection, description, control and access. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 830–832]

# The author’s main move

Zaagsma argues that digitised heritage is constituted through political decisions about what is selected, who controls digitisation, how materials are classified and described, and how access is mediated; these decisions shape what historians can know and research. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 830–845]

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Digital selectivity:** awareness that digitised resources represent selections rather than complete documentary records. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]
- **Explicit selection:** intentional inclusion/exclusion decisions in digitisation. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]
- **Implicit selection:** effects of search, retrieval and interface mechanisms on what users encounter. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 840]
- **Surgical discovery:** targeted retrieval that can bypass collection-level context. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, p. 844]
- **Digital archival literacy:** critical understanding of how digital resources are selected, structured, described and made accessible. [@Zaagsma2023DigitalHistoryPolitics, pp. 844–845]
- **Digital evidence surface:** my term for the subset of archival evidence made computationally available through digitisation, transcription, description and indexing.
- **Corpus provenance:** my extension of provenance to the construction and limits of that evidence surface.

# My response

Zaagsma is foundational for the thesis’s scoped-missingness rule because he makes the digital corpus itself an object of source criticism. The most important extension for DDR is to carry his chain one stage further: historical production → archival survival → cataloguing → digitisation → OCR/transcription → indexing/embedding → retrieval → synthesis. At each stage, absence can mean something different. The result is a stronger computational method because it makes unsupported historical absence claims harder to produce.

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** Zaagsma analyses digitisation broadly and does not directly study retrieval-augmented generation or embedding-based search.

**Risk if misused:** Treating every digital gap as politically motivated would overstate the argument; specific mechanisms and evidential conditions must be established.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Zaagsma connects archival mediation to computational mediation. Schwartz and Cook establish that archives are active sites of power; Duranti and Bearman show how description and context shape legibility; Moss, Thomas and Gollins show how archives are reconfigured as data; Mordell asks what is lost when archival records become computational objects; Bernard and Balog show how retrieval ranking further allocates visibility. For DDR, this creates a continuous chain from archival selection to computational selection. Scoped missingness is therefore not a technical disclaimer but a theoretical consequence of treating each transformation as an evidential boundary.
