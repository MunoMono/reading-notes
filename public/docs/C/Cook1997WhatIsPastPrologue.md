# Position + moment

Cook writes in the late 1990s from Canadian archival theory, reconstructing a century of professional thought from the Dutch Manual and Jenkinson through Schellenberg, Booms, Samuels, Taylor, Scott and electronic-records theory. The essay is deliberately historiographic and programmatic: it uses the history of ideas to argue for a future process- and context-centred paradigm. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 17–20, 43–49]

# The author’s main move

Cook argues that archival theory evolves with social and technological conditions and is moving toward a postcustodial paradigm centred on memory politics, functions, processes, enriched provenance and active archival shaping rather than passive custody of stable record products. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 17–49]

- **Claim (plain):** Archival theory is historically contingent rather than a fixed set of timeless principles.
- **Author claim:** Cook rejects a positivist intellectual history in which archival principles simply accumulate toward perfection.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 19–20 and 46 describe archival ideas as continually evolving in response to changes in records, organisations, technologies, users and wider intellectual trends.
- **Researcher inference:** Provenance, custody, appraisal and description should be read as historically changing concepts when applied to DDR.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook presents archival thinking as “constantly evolving” and relates its phases to broader social and intellectual contexts. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 19–20, 46]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archival principles are interpreted through the recordkeeping conditions they are asked to address.
- **Boundary:** Historical contingency does not mean archival principles lack continuity or evidential value.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should state which version of a concept such as provenance it is using.
- **Practice cross-check:** Distinguish traditional creator/provenance metadata from derived multi-relational research provenance.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Archives are “houses of memory” whose contents reflect politics of remembering and forgetting.
- **Author claim:** Cook opens by asking whose memory is preserved, whose is marginalised and how archival practices shape collective memory.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 17–19 frame archives as constructed sites of memory and point to historical exclusions, including women's experiences.
- **Researcher inference:** The DDR collection is not simply the department's memory; it is a selectively preserved and described memory structure.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook asks “whose memory?” and links archival formation to choices about inclusion, exclusion and silence. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 17–19]
- **Warrant (my words):** Collective memory depends on what institutions retain and make retrievable.
- **Boundary:** Absence in an archive does not establish deliberate exclusion without additional evidence.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should analyse mechanisms of omission without automatically attributing motive.
- **Practice cross-check:** Use feminist and missingness cases to distinguish sparse survival, descriptive obscurity and documented exclusion.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Archival justification has shifted from state administration toward wider societal memory and public use.
- **Author claim:** Cook identifies a move from juridical-administrative archives grounded in the state to socio-cultural archives grounded in broader public policy and use.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 43–44 describe archives as increasingly “of the people, for the people, even by the people.”
- **Researcher inference:** Activating DDR for wider research communities fits a broader archival shift toward public memory rather than merely institutional custody.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook's first concluding theme is the move from state-centred justification to social and cultural memory. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 43–44]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archives acquire public value when they support accountability, identity, culture and research beyond their creating administration.
- **Boundary:** Public use does not erase institutional rights, sensitivity or provenance.
- **Consequence:** DDR access/interface design should broaden use while preserving repository authority and rights constraints.
- **Practice cross-check:** Maintain RCA/V&A source rights and provenance even when records are surfaced through unified research views.

## Claim 4
- **Claim (plain):** Provenance should be understood through functions and multiple relationships, not only a single office of origin.
- **Author claim:** Cook traces a “rediscovery” and enrichment of provenance in Canadian and Australian theory, especially for complex and electronic records.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 35–39 and 47–48 argue for provenance around functions, activities, multiple creators and contextual relationships rather than static organisational attachment.
- **Researcher inference:** DDR relationships among projects, staff, units and records can be modelled archivally without reducing provenance to one hierarchy.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook describes provenance as a means of understanding the evidential context and interrelationships that turn information into knowledge. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 35–39, 47–48]
- **Warrant (my words):** Modern organisations and records often have distributed authorship and function, so a one-origin model obscures relevant context.
- **Boundary:** Research-derived relations are not automatically archival provenance; they require evidence.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should separate archival provenance from analytical relationship inference while linking both.
- **Practice cross-check:** Every rendered relational claim should bind to explicit source evidence and preserve creator/repository metadata.

## Claim 5
- **Claim (plain):** Archivists have become active shapers rather than passive keepers.
- **Author claim:** Cook argues that appraisal, description and recordkeeping intervention mean archivists shape archival heritage and its memory consequences.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 44–46 explicitly describe the profession moving from passive keepers of documentary residue to active shapers of archival heritage.
- **Researcher inference:** Computational selection, corpus boundaries and interface design likewise shape what DDR users encounter and therefore must be made accountable.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook says archivists now intervene actively in how records are appraised, preserved and contextualised. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 44–46]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archival neutrality becomes untenable once professional decisions determine what survives and how it is represented.
- **Boundary:** Active shaping does not grant licence to alter source content or invent context.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should treat its own transformations as documented interventions over immutable source records.
- **Practice cross-check:** Preserve corpus-generation receipts, excluded-scope rules and source/derived-data separation.

## Claim 6
- **Claim (plain):** The future archival paradigm moves from product and custody toward process, context and postcustodial control.
- **Author claim:** Cook proposes a shift from record as physical object toward functions, transactions and contextual linkages; custody may be distributed while archival intellectual control persists.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 47–49 explicitly describe a “postcustodial mindset” and movement “from a product-focused to a process-oriented activity.”
- **Researcher inference:** DDR computational activation can be framed as archival contextualisation when it remains provenance-aware and does not claim to replace the repositories or records.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cook calls for redefined provenance and contextual control suited to virtual, distributed and electronic records. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 47–49]
- **Warrant (my words):** The archival object becomes intelligible through the process and relationships that created and maintain it, not merely through physical custody.
- **Boundary:** Postcustodial theory has its own debates and does not mean physical preservation is unimportant.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should present its computational layer as a derived contextual research system over repository-held records.
- **Practice cross-check:** No ML/RAI derivative becomes the canonical source; users can return to PID-backed documents and repository provenance.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Houses of memory:** archives as socially constructed sites of collective remembering and forgetting. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 17–19]
- **Socio-cultural justification for archives:** public justification grounded in memory, identity, accountability and use beyond administration. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 43–44]
- **Active shapers of archival heritage:** archivists as participants in appraisal, description and memory formation rather than passive custodians. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 44–46]
- **Postcustodial mindset:** archival control centred on context, functions and processes even where physical custody is distributed. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 47–49]
- **Provenance refreshed:** provenance expanded toward functions, multiple relationships and contextual histories. [@Cook1997WhatIsPastPrologue, pp. 35–39, 47–48]

# My response

Cook gives the thesis an archival history that makes contextual and computational intervention intelligible as part of the field's development rather than an external technical add-on. His most useful concept for DDR is the move from product to process: the record remains important, but its meaning depends on functions, relationships and the decisions that shaped its survival. The caveat is that Cook's sweeping paradigm story should not substitute for evidence about the particular RCA/V&A systems or the thesis's own interventions.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Cook's process-oriented argument connects directly to Bearman's activity-centred documentation and Nesmith's later claim that archives have become foundations of knowledge. Duranti demonstrates historically why descriptive forms change, while Ketelaar shows records continuing to acquire meaning through activation. For DDR, Cook supplies the bridge: contemporary relational and computational views can be archivally legitimate as contextual interventions, but their shaping power must itself be documented and kept distinct from source evidence.
