# Position + moment

Cifor and Wood write in 2017 within the emerging field of critical archival studies. They review feminist archival movements, feminist theory within archival studies and the wider “archival turn” in humanities scholarship, then argue for an explicitly intersectional and transformative feminist praxis. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 1–3]

# The author’s main move

They argue that feminist archival work must move beyond better representation of women toward transformation of archival institutions, professional assumptions and knowledge structures through intersectional critique, situated knowledge, care and affect. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 1–27]

# Six-claim evidence ledger

## Claim 1
- **Claim (plain):** Critical feminism in archives must go beyond representation.
- **Author claim:** Cifor and Wood argue that much feminist archival work has focused on collecting women and improving representation, but that critical feminism demands institutional transformation.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** The introduction defines critical feminism as an intersectional political philosophy directed at dismantling heteronormative, capitalist and racist patriarchy and explicitly rejects representation as a sufficient endpoint. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 1–3]
- **Researcher inference:** Counting women in DDR is necessary but insufficient; the thesis must also examine how value, authorship, support labour and institutional categories were structured.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors say archival theory and practice have yet to fully engage a feminist praxis aimed at more than “attaining better representation of women in archives.” [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 1–3]
- **Warrant (my words):** A collection can contain women while still reproducing structures that subordinate their work or make it difficult to recognise.
- **Boundary:** The paper's transformative political programme is broader than what one historical case study can demonstrate.
- **Consequence:** DDR feminist analysis should examine mechanisms, not merely presence/absence counts.
- **Practice cross-check:** Compare authorship, role labels, acknowledgements, staff lists and project contribution for Patterson, Daley and other women.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Feminist and other social movements have used archives as tools of self-representation and self-historicisation.
- **Author claim:** The authors trace feminist, LGBTQ and racialised community archives as counter-archival responses to exclusion and misrepresentation.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Their historical section shows activist archives emerging from movements seeking continuity, visibility and control over how their histories were represented. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 4–7]
- **Researcher inference:** Institutional archives should be read alongside the possibility that some groups' experiences were documented elsewhere, informally or not at all.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cifor and Wood describe counter-archives as resisting expectations of archives as neutral/passive repositories and exposing exclusion and silence. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 4–7]
- **Warrant (my words):** Self-documentation responds to the power institutions exercise over whose history becomes durable.
- **Boundary:** DDR staff and students did not necessarily form counter-archives, and no such structure should be inferred without evidence.
- **Consequence:** Oral histories and personal papers can be treated as potentially different archival positions rather than merely supplementary sources.
- **Practice cross-check:** Search interviewees' private or published traces only where consent/access permits and keep provenance distinct.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Feminist archival theory challenges claims of neutrality and universal objectivity.
- **Author claim:** Cifor and Wood review feminist critiques of archival professionalism and draw on Haraway's situated knowledge to replace view-from-nowhere objectivity.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Their discussion of Schwartz and Cook explicitly uses gendered archival practice to contest neutrality and describes situated knowledge as accounting for the agency of the knowledge producer. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 8–11]
- **Researcher inference:** The thesis's own archive selection, metadata mapping and computational interpretation should be positioned and documented rather than presented as neutral discovery.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The article connects feminist objectivity with the recognition that archival knowledge is produced from situated positions. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 8–11]
- **Warrant (my words):** Acknowledging situatedness can strengthen accountability because it exposes the conditions under which claims were produced.
- **Boundary:** Situated knowledge is not permission to treat all interpretations as equally supported.
- **Consequence:** The researcher should state decisions, constraints and evidential standards explicitly.
- **Practice cross-check:** Preserve corpus boundaries, selection rules, model versions and researcher-generated categories.

## Claim 4
- **Claim (plain):** Feminist ethics of care and affect change how archival relationships are conceptualised.
- **Author claim:** The authors review work that frames archivists as caregivers bound to creators, subjects, users and communities through affective responsibility.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** The middle section describes feminist ethics of care and affect as expanding social-justice thinking beyond procedural neutrality. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 11–13]
- **Researcher inference:** DDR records concerning living people and interview participants should be handled as relational evidence involving subjects, not only data objects.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The paper cites a “web of mutual affective responsibility” linking archival actors. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 11–13]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archival decisions can affect represented people and communities even when records are formally public.
- **Boundary:** Care must not become paternalism or override legitimate scholarly critique.
- **Consequence:** Ethical decisions should balance evidence, consent, harm and public-interest considerations.
- **Practice cross-check:** Keep oral-history consent, living-person handling and evidential quotation policies explicit.

## Claim 5
- **Claim (plain):** The wider archival turn reveals archives as agents structured by inclusion, exclusion, silence and representation.
- **Author claim:** Cifor and Wood review humanities scholarship that treats the archive as apparatus, discourse and site of structural power rather than neutral container.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** The archival-turn section highlights archives as agents organised around logics of inclusion/exclusion and shaped by colonial and disciplinary power. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 15–20]
- **Researcher inference:** The DDR archive's organisation is part of the design-historical object of study, not only the means of accessing it.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors characterise the archive as “steeped in power” and organised through particular logics of silence and representation. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 15–20]
- **Warrant (my words):** Historical narratives inherit the structures through which evidence was organised and made retrievable.
- **Boundary:** “Archive” as metaphor in humanities theory can drift away from actual archival practice; the authors themselves note blind spots across fields.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should connect theory to concrete catalogue, series and record-level mechanisms.
- **Practice cross-check:** Use archive metadata and document structures to evidence claims about visibility or obscurity.

## Claim 6
- **Claim (plain):** Intersectionality and affect should reshape archival professionalism and community concepts.
- **Author claim:** In their final section, the authors argue that archival studies has often addressed identity groups separately and needs intersectional approaches that examine how communities and professional structures are produced.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 20–26 discuss gender neutrality in hierarchy, gaps in intersectionality across professional categories, and affective relations that form communities.
- **Researcher inference:** DDR gender analysis should not assume “women” is a homogeneous category or separate gender from role, status, discipline and institutional position.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Cifor and Wood call for intersectional analysis of archival scholarship, practice and professionalism and for attention to affective community formation. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 20–26]
- **Warrant (my words):** Visibility and marginalisation emerge through interacting institutional positions, not one identity variable in isolation.
- **Boundary:** The DDR archive may not preserve sufficient demographic information for full intersectional analysis.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should state where intersectional questions cannot be responsibly answered from the corpus.
- **Practice cross-check:** Do not infer protected characteristics; analyse only evidenced role, authorship, labour and testimony.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Critical feminism:** intersectional feminist praxis aimed at structural transformation rather than representation alone. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 1–3]
- **Counter-archive:** archival practice used by communities to self-represent and contest dominant historical structures. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 4–7]
- **Situated archival knowledge:** knowledge whose producer, position and conditions are made explicit rather than hidden behind neutrality. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 8–11]
- **Affective responsibility:** relational ethical responsibility linking record creators, subjects, archivists, users and communities. [@Cifor2017CriticalFeminismArchives, pp. 11–13]

# My response

Cifor and Wood materially strengthen the DDR feminist strand because they shift the question from “where are the women?” to “what archival and historiographic structures determine how different kinds of contribution become recognisable?” That aligns closely with Buckley while moving the critique into archival systems, care and situated knowledge. It also sets a useful limit: the thesis can interrogate gendered labour and authorship where evidence survives, but it should not infer intersectional identities that the archive does not establish.
