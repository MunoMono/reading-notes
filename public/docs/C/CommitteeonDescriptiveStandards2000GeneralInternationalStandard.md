# Position + moment

The second edition of ISAD(G), adopted by the ICA Committee on Descriptive Standards in 1999 and published in 2000, provides general guidance for archival description and is designed to work with national standards. It defines twenty-six possible descriptive elements, multilevel rules and a description-control area. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 7–12, 34–35]

# The source’s main move

ISAD(G) formalises archival description as a contextual, hierarchical and revisable representation of archival material. It connects identity, creator, custody, content, arrangement, access, related materials and the provenance of the description itself so that records can be identified and retrieved without detaching parts from the larger fonds to which they belong. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 7–12]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Archival description is an active representational process rather than a transparent label attached after the fact. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) states that archival description identifies and explains the context and content of archival material in order to promote accessibility, and that description-related processes can begin at or before creation and continue throughout the life of records. **Evidence.** The introduction describes archival descriptions as “accurate and appropriate representations,” notes that descriptive information remains dynamic and may be amended as knowledge changes, and links description to intellectual control. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, p. 7] **Evidence-supported claim.** The standard itself treats description as a continuing process of representation and control, not as neutral transcription. **Researcher inference.** DDR catalogue metadata should therefore be analysed as a historically and institutionally produced layer of evidence about the records, not as the records themselves. **Warrant.** If description can be created, integrated, selected, updated and amended, its form reflects documentary and archival decisions. **Boundary.** ISAD(G) aims for accurate and appropriate description; it does not present description primarily as ideological mediation. **Consequence.** The thesis should keep catalogue description distinct from documentary content while preserving both as inspectable evidence. **Practice cross-check.** Separate source passage, catalogue metadata and generated synthesis in the research instrument.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Context is structurally prioritised through multilevel description. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) requires descriptions to proceed from the general to the specific and to represent parts in hierarchical relation to the fonds as a whole. **Evidence.** The standard links this rule to respect des fonds and specifies fonds, series, file and item levels whose descriptions are meaningful in relation to higher levels. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 8, 12] **Evidence-supported claim.** An item’s descriptive meaning is not self-sufficient; its archival position supplies context that should survive retrieval. **Researcher inference.** DDR computational retrieval should not surface isolated chunks without retaining document and higher-level archival identity. **Warrant.** A passage detached from its fonds/series/file context loses information that ISAD(G) treats as constitutive of archival description. **Boundary.** Hierarchical description does not capture every possible intellectual or historical relationship among records. **Consequence.** The digital corpus should preserve parent-child links and distinguish semantic proximity from archival relationship. **Practice cross-check.** Require PID/document identity and catalogue context alongside retrieved text chunks.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Description is intentionally level-specific and non-redundant, which means absence of detail at one level cannot be read as absence in the archive. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) instructs archivists to provide only information appropriate to the level being described and not to repeat information already given at a higher level. **Evidence.** Rules 2.2 and 2.4 explicitly limit detailed content at fonds level and avoid repetition across hierarchically related descriptions. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, p. 12] **Evidence-supported claim.** Sparse item- or file-level metadata may reflect descriptive design rather than lack of underlying content or importance. **Researcher inference.** Missing names or topics in DDR catalogue entries cannot automatically support claims that the underlying records do not contain them. **Warrant.** The standard deliberately distributes information unevenly across levels. **Boundary.** This does not explain every omission; some gaps may result from appraisal, limited resources or past descriptive choices. **Consequence.** Scoped missingness must distinguish “not retrieved/not described here” from “not present in the defined corpus.” **Practice cross-check.** Compare item-level and parent-level metadata before treating catalogue silence as evidential absence.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Archival description is selective even within a formal standard. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) offers twenty-six elements but requires only a small subset for international exchange: reference code, title, creator, dates, extent and level of description. **Evidence.** The standard states that all elements are available but only a subset need be used in any description and that the degree of additional information varies with the unit described. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, p. 9] **Evidence-supported claim.** Standardised description guarantees a minimum descriptive structure, not exhaustive representation of all people, actions or concepts in the records. **Researcher inference.** DDR metadata should be treated as an access surface shaped by selective descriptive priorities rather than a comprehensive index of design knowledge. **Warrant.** A field omitted from the standard minimum cannot be presumed to have been captured consistently. **Boundary.** Local standards and repository practice may add much richer description. **Consequence.** Computational analysis should use metadata as one evidential layer while returning to source text for substantive historical claims. **Practice cross-check.** Audit which DDR entities are strongly represented in catalogue fields versus only in document text.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Creator and authority structures directly influence archival discoverability. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) states that access points are based on descriptive elements and that authority control increases their value for retrieval; it points to ISAAR(CPF) for corporate bodies, persons and families named as creators. **Evidence.** The standard explicitly links access points, authority records and retrieval and visually models relationships between creators and units of description. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 8–9] **Evidence-supported claim.** Controlled creator identities and access points shape which records can be grouped and found through named entities. **Researcher inference.** DDR authority reconciliation is not merely data cleaning: it changes the legibility of people, roles and project relationships. **Warrant.** Retrieval quality depends partly on how creator identities are represented and linked. **Boundary.** Creator authority does not establish who performed every action recorded in a document. **Consequence.** The thesis must preserve the distinction between catalogue association, recorded action and inferred role. **Practice cross-check.** Use authority-provenance groups while preventing creator metadata from automatically becoming evidence of action.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Archival history and appraisal are interpretative context because they explain how the surviving evidence acquired its present form. **Author claim.** ISAD(G) instructs descriptions to record transfers, custody, arrangement, reuse and migrations that affect present structure, and to record appraisal, destruction and scheduling where these actions may affect interpretation. **Evidence.** Sections 3.2.3 and 3.3.2 explicitly connect archival history and appraisal/destruction to authenticity, integrity and interpretation. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 20, 24] **Evidence-supported claim.** Selection, destruction, arrangement and custody are part of the evidential context of archival material. **Researcher inference.** DDR missingness cannot be interpreted solely at the level of digitised search results; survival, arrangement and digitisation history may also constrain what can be established. **Warrant.** The standard itself recognises that actions performed on records can alter their structure and interpretation. **Boundary.** ISAD(G) records these processes when known; it cannot recover undocumented losses or decisions. **Consequence.** Thesis claims about absence should remain scoped to the available evidence surface. **Practice cross-check.** Keep corpus inclusion/exclusion rules separate from claims about historical non-existence.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** The provenance of archival description is itself describable and therefore open to scrutiny. **Author claim.** The Description Control Area records how, when and by whom a description was prepared and which rules or conventions governed it. **Evidence.** Sections 3.7.1–3.7.3 require an archivist’s note, rules/conventions and dates of description or revision. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 34–35] **Evidence-supported claim.** A catalogue description has its own authorship, methodological basis and revision history. **Researcher inference.** Computationally derived descriptions, labels and syntheses in the DDR instrument should carry comparable provenance so their production is not mistaken for original archival description. **Warrant.** If human archival description is controlled through explicit provenance, derived machine representations should not be exempt from equivalent accountability. **Boundary.** ISAD(G) does not prescribe provenance for AI-generated representations. **Consequence.** The thesis can use ISAD(G) as a bounded analogy for exposing who/what produced a derived research representation and when. **Practice cross-check.** Record model/version, source passages and creation action for selected generated outputs.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Archival description →** a structured, contextual and revisable representation used to identify, manage, locate and explain archival material and its record-producing context. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 7, 10]
- **Provenance →** the relationship between records and the organisations or individuals that created, accumulated, maintained and used them in activity. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, p. 11]
- **Multilevel description →** linked hierarchical description from fonds toward more specific levels, with information appropriate to each level and minimal repetition. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, p. 12]
- **Description control →** information about who prepared a description, which rules were used and when it was prepared or revised. [@CommitteeonDescriptiveStandards2000GeneralInternationalStandard, pp. 34–35]

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** ISAD(G) is a descriptive standard, not a critical theory of power, silence or algorithmic mediation.

**Risk:** Treating alignment with ISAD(G) as proof of completeness or neutrality would overstate what a descriptive standard can establish. In this thesis it is a mapping and provenance reference, not institutional certification.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

ISAD(G) establishes the operational logic of archival description: hierarchy, context, creator, appraisal history, access and description control. Harris’s *Archival Sliver* makes the critical intervention that those same archival processes are never outside power, while Aske and Giardinetti show how legacy descriptions and controlled vocabularies become training material and access conditions for contemporary AI. Together they make a productive three-part lens for DDR: preserve the archival structure, interrogate the power and selection embedded in it, and prevent computational enrichment from erasing the history of description. What remains to be demonstrated empirically is where DDR catalogue structure helps interpretation and where source-level evidence complicates or exceeds the descriptive surface.
