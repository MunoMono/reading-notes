# Position + moment (2–4 lines)
Gilliland and Caswell write from critical archival studies, human rights research, and archival affect in the mid-2010s, when archival theory was increasingly engaging with absence, emotion, community memory, and nondominant epistemologies. Their intervention is explicitly against dominant archival preoccupation with the actual, accessible, and evidentially deployable record. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55–57, 71–72]`
**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):** This essay is not just about archival absence in general. Its stronger move is to argue that imagined records can have real social force and that archival theory must account for affect, aspiration, and impossible futures, not only for extant evidence. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55, 61, 71–72]`

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)
They argue that archival theory must account for impossible archival imaginaries and imagined records in order to explain how archives shape affect, aspiration, and political possibility even when the desired records are absent, inaccessible, or never actualized.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Their positive proposal is that archives must take affect and alternate epistemologies seriously, because truth and authority may live partly in aspiration, grief, and social meaning rather than only in extant facts.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** In the conclusion they argue that archival theory must complicate the link between record and event to accommodate records “collectively conjured by affect rather than created by event.” They say this move challenges dominant notions of evidence and opens archival thinking to nondominant and pluralist epistemologies. They also insist that archival practice should take affect seriously and find ways for description, retrieval, and use to account for absences and their “attending affects.” `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 71–72]`

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)
- **Impossible archival imaginaries:** archival imaginaries operating where the archive and its hoped-for contents are absent or forever unattainable; I will use this for situations where the DDR archive is shaped by anticipated but unavailable evidence. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55, 61]`
- **Imagined records:** records that do not actually exist in the imagined form but function socially because of affect, aspiration, or the weight of absence; I will use this where later readers or communities project explanatory force onto missing DDR documentation. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55, 57, 71]`
- **Archival affect:** the emotional and social force attached to records and absences, including hope, distrust, frustration, longing, and trauma; I will use this to read archival silence as active rather than empty. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55–56, 61–64]`
- **Continuum of recordkeeping affect:** the shifting movement through which internal emotions and external circumstances shape how records and absences are experienced; useful for reading changing relations to the DDR archive over time. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, p. 61]`
- **Actualized / non-actualized records:** the distinction between records materially instantiated and those imagined but socially consequential; I will use this cautiously to separate archival presence from archival force. `[@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 57, 61, 71]`
