# Thesis job

**How this source moves the primary research question forward:** Gilliland and Caswell broaden archival theory beyond extant records by analysing the force of imagined, absent and unattainable records. This helps the thesis distinguish legitimate analysis of archival absence from speculative historical completion.

**How this source bears on the secondary question:** The paper shows that records and their absences can shape affect, identity and political possibility, which matters when revisiting DDR ideas whose documentary traces are uneven or incomplete.

**Why I’m reading this now:** It offers a rigorous vocabulary for thinking about what researchers expect or wish an archive could contain without turning those expectations into evidence.

**Where it sits in my argument:** Canon + intellectual lineage. It brings affect, human rights and plural epistemologies into archival theory.

**My benchmark for using it:** Imagined records may be historically consequential as evidence of affect or aspiration, but they will never be used as substitutes for missing DDR documents.

# Position + moment

Gilliland and Caswell write from archival studies and human-rights research. They explicitly challenge archival theory's preference for the actual, instantiated and accessible by introducing “impossible archival imaginaries” and “imagined records” as phenomena with real affective and social effects despite their absence from the material record. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 53–56]

# The author’s main move

They argue that archives must account for the meanings and effects generated by absent, unattainable or imagined records, while distinguishing those phenomena from conventional legal, bureaucratic and historical evidence. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 53–75]

# Six-claim evidence ledger

## Claim 1
- **Claim (plain):** Archival absence can itself generate socially consequential meaning.
- **Author claim:** The authors begin from cases in which anticipated records are unavailable or fail to contain what people expected, yet narratives and affect surrounding that absence persist.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** The Garbo/de Acosta example shows how “no evidence” becomes part of an archive story and how speculation can continue to shape interpretation. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 54–55]
- **Researcher inference:** The absence of a DDR record may be historically meaningful as a condition of later interpretation without proving that a particular event or contribution occurred.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors describe the “nothing” or “no evidence” encountered in an archive as capable of becoming evidence of another sort — evidence about expectation and affect. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 54–55]
- **Warrant (my words):** Researchers' expectations and frustrations are historical/social phenomena even when the anticipated record does not exist.
- **Boundary:** The absence cannot be converted into evidence for the factual content that researchers hoped to find.
- **Consequence:** Scoped missingness can report what the corpus fails to establish while avoiding speculative completion.
- **Practice cross-check:** Missing DDR evidence should trigger a boundary statement, not an inferred event or relationship.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Dominant archival theory privileges actual, accessible records and under-theorises affect.
- **Author claim:** Gilliland and Caswell argue that archival practice is preoccupied with records that have presence, established evidentiary capacity and identifiable users.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 55–56 say archival theory offers little conceptual space for the content or archive as imagined and for the affect associated with absence.
- **Researcher inference:** A DDR framework focused only on retrievable documents would miss how later participants experience institutional omission, recognition or loss.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The paper calls for explicit acknowledgement of imaginings about absent or unattainable archives. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55–56]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archival significance includes emotional and political responses to what records fail to provide.
- **Boundary:** Affect should not be treated as proof of disputed historical facts.
- **Consequence:** Oral histories can capture experiences of archival absence while remaining a distinct evidence class.
- **Practice cross-check:** Ask interviewees about absence/recognition but code those statements as testimony rather than contemporary documentation.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** “Imagined records” can function as affective counter-evidence without being factual records.
- **Author claim:** The authors argue that imagined records may address survivors' affective needs or challenge official narratives even though they cannot be cross-examined or establish events in conventional evidentiary terms.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Their human-rights discussion says imaginary documents broaden documentation toward affective needs while remaining unable to provide conventional proof of atrocities. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 56–58]
- **Researcher inference:** DDR research can analyse stories about the record people believe should exist, but must preserve the distinction between aspirational/remembered evidence and surviving documentary evidence.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The paper explicitly separates the social value of imagined documentation from conventional legal or bureaucratic evidence. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 56–58]
- **Warrant (my words):** Something can have historical consequences as an imagined object without being materially authentic.
- **Boundary:** “Counter-evidence” here is affective/political, not evidentiary equivalence.
- **Consequence:** The thesis must not let generative synthesis materialise the record that participants wish had existed.
- **Practice cross-check:** RAI should return scoped missingness where a hypothesised record is unsupported.

## Claim 4
- **Claim (plain):** Archival imaginaries are shaped by power and must be analysed by asking whose imaginary is being represented.
- **Author claim:** Drawing on scholarship on social imaginaries, the authors stress heterogeneity and the need to identify whose imagined archive is at stake.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Their theoretical review explicitly cites the caution “Whose imaginary is this?” and warns against treating communities as homogeneous. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 59–62]
- **Researcher inference:** A DDR “institutional memory” cannot be treated as one collective voice; Archer, staff, students, administrators and later historians may imagine the department differently.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The paper presents imaginaries as socially situated and capable of being tools of both oppression and liberation. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 59–62]
- **Warrant (my words):** Collective memory categories can suppress internal conflict if their authorship and constituency are hidden.
- **Boundary:** The paper does not provide a ready-made method for mapping every constituency in a historical department.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should attribute retrospective narratives to named speakers/groups and preserve disagreement.
- **Practice cross-check:** Oral-history synthesis should retain individual testimony rather than produce one “DDR remembered” narrative.

## Claim 5
- **Claim (plain):** Imagined records can interact with extant records to produce alternate or new realities.
- **Author claim:** The authors describe cases where official records and imagined records confront one another or co-constitute new social possibilities.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Near the conclusion they say imagined records can arise where official evidence is deemed erroneous or failed, sometimes standing against “the establishment” and sometimes opening possible futures. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 69–72]
- **Researcher inference:** Archive activation can change contemporary understanding of DDR, but this change must be described as new interpretation rather than recovered historical certainty.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The paper states that actual and imagined records may confront each other with alternate realities or interact to co-constitute new realities. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 69–72]
- **Warrant (my words):** Archives participate in present social meaning as well as retrospective factual reconstruction.
- **Boundary:** Contemporary effect is not proof that a retrospective interpretation is historically correct.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should distinguish what activation *does now* from what the archival record establishes *then*.
- **Practice cross-check:** Separate evidential outcome from interpretative/heritage impact in findings.

## Claim 6
- **Claim (plain):** Archival theory should accommodate plural epistemologies without giving up the distinction between fact and affect.
- **Author claim:** The conclusion calls for openness to alternate constructions of record authority and explicit consideration of affect.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 71–72 argue that impossible records can embody collective grief and aspiration and that archival theory should complicate the link between record and event.
- **Researcher inference:** Critical archival theory can widen what the thesis attends to while the research method still enforces strong evidential boundaries.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors say archival theory can no longer afford to ignore imaginative phenomena and that doing so opens archival thinking to nondominant and pluralist epistemologies. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 71–72]
- **Warrant (my words):** Epistemic plurality requires recognising different functions of records, not erasing distinctions among those functions.
- **Boundary:** The article intentionally unsettles conventional evidence theory; it does not offer a formal protocol for adjudicating disputed claims.
- **Consequence:** DDR analysis should allow affective/testimonial significance while reserving factual historical claims for adequately supported evidence.
- **Practice cross-check:** Use explicit labels for source, testimony, synthesis and missingness.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Impossible archival imaginary:** an individually or collectively imagined archive that cannot be materially realised but has affective and social force. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55–72]
- **Imagined record:** a record conceived in response to absence, aspiration, distrust or unmet evidential needs, despite not existing as an extant record. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 56–72]
- **Archival affect:** emotional and embodied response to records and their absences that can influence cognition and action. [@Gilliland2016RecordsImaginaries, pp. 55–56]

# My response

Gilliland and Caswell are useful precisely because they give absence theoretical weight without licensing historical invention. Their strongest contribution to DDR is the distinction between a missing record and the social work done by the idea of that record. This sits very well with scoped missingness: the thesis can analyse why an absence matters, how it is remembered and what it causes researchers or participants to imagine, while refusing to turn that imagined content into evidence.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Gilliland and Caswell radicalise the problem of absence that critical archival studies raises more broadly. Ketelaar shows records being repeatedly activated; Gilliland and Caswell show that even absent records can acquire affective force. Duranti and Bearman, by contrast, focus on systems that make extant records describable and contextual. Together they provide a useful DDR distinction: a trace can be materially present, descriptively obscured, absent but remembered, or wholly imagined. Those states must never be collapsed into one evidential category.
