# Position + moment

Mordell writes in 2019 as computational archival science and “collections as data” are emerging as significant professional agendas. Her intervention is deliberately early and diagnostic: she asks what ideological baggage enters when digital archives are reframed as data and when computational methods become normal archival infrastructure. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 140–146]

# The author’s main move

Mordell argues that archives do not become neutral data merely because they are digital: datafication is a historically situated transformation whose concepts, tools, training data and interfaces can revive older myths of objectivity unless decision-making, provenance, plurality and ethics remain explicit. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 144–161]

# Six-claim evidence ledger

## Claim 1
- **Claim (plain):** Digital archives become data through datafication; digitisation alone does not make them computational datasets.
- **Author claim:** Mordell distinguishes the record remaining intelligible for preservation/access from the act of preparing archival materials for computational manipulation.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 144–146 explicitly state that digital archives are not already data “by virtue of being digital” but become datafied through acts that make them computationally tractable; she describes this as potentially a new archival paradigm.
- **Researcher inference:** The DDR vector corpus is not the archive itself but a derived computational evidence surface produced through specific transformations.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell differentiates digitisation from datafication and says computational use represents a distinct analytical stance. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 144–146]
- **Warrant (my words):** If computation requires additional preparation, those preparation steps mediate the evidence before analysis begins.
- **Boundary:** Datafication can be methodologically legitimate and useful; the point is to make its operations visible.
- **Consequence:** Corpus construction, chunking and derived metadata should be documented as transformations rather than treated as transparent access.
- **Practice cross-check:** The DDR workflow preserves canonical source identifiers and records release-level corpus transformations.

## Claim 2
- **Claim (plain):** Archives-as-data can revive myths of neutrality and objectivity.
- **Author claim:** Mordell links big-data rhetoric to an “aura” of truth, objectivity and quantitative authority and warns that archival neutrality may return in computational form.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 148–151 examine both objectivity and the metaphor of “raw data,” arguing that data are never raw and that computational framing can conceal historical and professional intervention.
- **Researcher inference:** Embedding maps, ranked retrieval and generated synthesis should not be presented as though patterns simply emerge untouched from DDR records.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell treats “raw data” as an oxymoron and connects the language of data to earlier organicist archival ideas that naturalised order. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 148–151]
- **Warrant (my words):** Technical representation can inherit epistemic authority from the language of data even when human selection and classification remain substantial.
- **Boundary:** Quantitative/computational analysis is not inherently positivist; the risk arises when its assumptions and transformations disappear.
- **Consequence:** Visual and model outputs need explicit methodological framing and provenance.
- **Practice cross-check:** UMAP is presented as a derived semantic projection rather than an objective map of the archive.

## Claim 3
- **Claim (plain):** Computational archival tools can hide human judgement behind apparently automatic interfaces.
- **Author claim:** Mordell uses the “Devil’s Bridge” metaphor to describe computational infrastructure whose human labour and design assumptions disappear from view.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 151–153 argue that software and algorithms are designed by people and that archival NLP involves interpretive decisions by both archivists and developers.
- **Researcher inference:** The DDR interface must expose the chain between retrieval settings, model behaviour and historical claims rather than allowing automation to appear self-authorising.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell describes computational arrangement and description as capable of making the human work behind an interface invisible. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 151–153]
- **Warrant (my words):** Apparent automation can transfer authority from visible archival judgement to opaque technical systems without reducing the amount of judgement involved.
- **Boundary:** Opacity is not inevitable; open tools and documented pipelines can make decisions inspectable.
- **Consequence:** Explainability should be designed into the workflow rather than added after model output.
- **Practice cross-check:** DDR RAI exposes evidence cards, source usage and provenance bindings instead of output-only answers.

## Claim 4
- **Claim (plain):** NLP and other computational methods privilege records that match their training data and technical assumptions.
- **Author claim:** Mordell argues that model “brittleness,” language bias and training-corpus composition affect which archival materials are computationally legible.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 153–155 discuss NLP systems trained on limited corpora, Anglo-centric bias, diacritic “cleaning,” and the danger that “quick wins” privilege collections easiest for existing systems to parse.
- **Researcher inference:** Retrieval quality in DDR may vary systematically across record genres, naming conventions or OCR quality, so absence from model results cannot be equated with absence from the archive.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell warns that out-of-the-box NLP is likely to privilege records more computationally legible to a particular system. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 153–155]
- **Warrant (my words):** Model accessibility is partly a property of how well historical material fits contemporary technical representations.
- **Boundary:** The specific biases of bge-m3/Qwen or the DDR corpus require empirical testing; Mordell does not establish them in advance.
- **Consequence:** Computational missingness should be tested against metadata and direct source retrieval.
- **Practice cross-check:** UAT includes scoped missingness and source-level verification rather than trusting vector ranking alone.

## Claim 5
- **Claim (plain):** Datafication adds a new layer of abstraction that archivists must actively interpret and explain.
- **Author claim:** Mordell argues that computational processing can enhance access but requires archival professionals to engage in tool development, critique system design and explain methods in plain language.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 155–156 explicitly say that computational arrangement/description creates an additional abstraction layer and that archivists should critically question design while communicating what systems do.
- **Researcher inference:** The DDR computational strand should remain archivally/historically governed rather than delegated to technical optimisation alone.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell argues that access-enhancing computational methods require active professional involvement and explanation. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 155–156]
- **Warrant (my words):** Domain expertise is required to recognise when technically plausible operations alter evidential meaning.
- **Boundary:** One researcher cannot reproduce the institutional role of a professional archival team.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should define its technical choices against explicit archival and historiographic requirements.
- **Practice cross-check:** Frozen scope, evidence hierarchy and UAT criteria constrain implementation choices.

## Claim 6
- **Claim (plain):** Computational archival science should be plural, decision-visible, provenance-aware and ethically reflexive.
- **Author claim:** Mordell proposes concrete interventions: pluralising CAS participation, keeping decision processes visible, documenting data provenance, revisiting professional ethics and collaborating with critical data/decolonial computing fields.
- **Evidence-supported claim:** Pages 157–160 set out these provocations and argue that the emerging field can still be shaped before practices become entrenched.
- **Researcher inference:** A responsible DDR computational framework should treat provenance and inclusion as core architecture rather than contextual discussion around a finished system.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** Mordell calls for documentation of data cleaning and interpretive decisions and for power/privilege critique to be incorporated into CAS. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 157–160]
- **Warrant (my words):** If computational practice is still being institutionalised, design decisions can embed either transparency or opacity as defaults.
- **Boundary:** Mordell's proposals are normative directions, not evidence that any particular architecture is sufficient.
- **Consequence:** The thesis should map each responsible-AI/archival control to a concrete computational risk.
- **Practice cross-check:** Provenance bindings, scoped missingness, versioned corpus releases and explicit model limitations directly operationalise this direction.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Datafication:** transformation of digitised archival material into forms made computationally tractable for analysis. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 144–146]
- **Archives-as-data paradigm:** emerging archival mindset in which computational operations become central to how archives are conceptualised and used. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 145–146]
- **Computational legibility:** my shorthand for the uneven ease with which records can be parsed, represented and surfaced by a given computational system, grounded in Mordell's discussion of training corpora, language and “quick wins.” [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, pp. 153–155]
- **Data provenance:** documentation of where data came from and what cleaning/interpretive operations have been performed on it. [@Mordell2019CriticalQuestionsArchivesData, p. 159]

# My response

Mordell is foundational because she makes the computational transformation itself visible. The article does not ask whether computation is good or bad; it asks what happens when the archive is redescribed through the language and infrastructure of data. That move fits DDR particularly well because the research system deliberately creates a new semantic evidence surface. The theoretical requirement is therefore to keep that surface derivative, documented and contestable rather than letting it masquerade as the archive itself.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Mordell bridges Bowker and Star's classification critique, Cook's contextual archival paradigm and contemporary computational archival science. Drucker similarly insists that digital-humanities representations are constructed rather than given, while later sources such as Jaillant and Aske, Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan, and Bender et al. specify practical risks around infrastructure, data and model behaviour. For DDR, Mordell therefore provides the canonical hinge: digitisation creates access, but datafication creates a new interpretative layer whose provenance, exclusions and design decisions must itself become part of the evidence story.
