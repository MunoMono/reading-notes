# Thesis job

**How this advances the primary research question:** Mackenzie argues that machine learners *institute ways of recognising difference* through the operations by which they split, stabilise, bend, blur and multiply classifications. I can apply this to ask what the DDR instrument's embedding, retrieval and visualisation operations make perceptible as an archival relationship, and what historical judgement is needed before promoting a computational grouping to a claim about the past.

**Relevance to the secondary question:** New groupings of documentary traces may bring neglected or contested design ideas into conversation. Their value to design research today, however, cannot be established from modelled proximity, partition or classification alone.

**Why this chapter follows Chapters 1–5:** Chapters 1–2 examine machine learning as a heterogeneous, diagrammatic research practice; Chapter 3 examines constructed vector space; Chapter 4 examines operational functions, observation and optimisation; Chapter 5 examines probabilisation and the persistence of error even with extensive data. Chapter 6 asks what these practices *do to difference*, using decision trees and support vector machines to show why a 'pattern' is not a fixed or innocent object.

**My benchmark:** (1) A displayed DDR grouping must be describable in terms of the actual model and interface operations that produced it, with sources reachable for inspection. (2) Where a historical claim relies on a cluster or ranked relationship, I must investigate difficult near matches, exclusions and competing source interpretations before treating it as supported.

# Position and the author's main move

Mackenzie opens with a question about machine learning's accountability for the patterns it claims to recognise, then proposes that what counts as pattern changes with the techniques used to differentiate data (pp. 125–127). He uses a material comparison rather than an abstract denunciation: **decision trees** recursively *split* data according to a hierarchy of features and later limit those splits through pruning (pp. 127–137); **support vector machines** attend to hard-to-separate cases and use margins and higher-dimensional feature mappings to establish boundaries that ordinary linear separations would not produce (pp. 137–149). The concluding claim is that differences of *degree*, expressed through calculable geometry, can obscure or rearrange differences of *kind*, while nevertheless creating opportunities for critical investigation (p. 149).

**Source-specific limit:** The methods worked through here are historical decision trees, CART, random forests, support vector machines and their illustrative datasets. They are **not** the DDR embedding model bge-m3, UMAP's graph-manifold procedure or a retrieval-augmented language model. Their value to this thesis is a critically tested *analogy about making patterns*, not a licence to transfer their technical mechanisms to another system.

## Claim 1 — 'Pattern' has no fixed operational meaning apart from the practice that recognises it

**Author claim:** Machine learning can alter what counts as a pattern and, therefore, how difference is articulated. **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie asks whether learners create new patterns of difference and whether their pattern claims deserve scrutiny (p. 125), then says that machine learning has no single operational definition of *pattern* and proposes examining its specific operations of differentiation instead (pp. 126–127). In the conclusion, he describes grouping as heterogeneous across technical systems (p. 149). **Warrant:** If a pattern is constituted differently by partitioning, geometric margins or ensembles, its apparent identity cannot be explained by saying only that the algorithm 'found' it. **My inference:** A DDR semantic neighbourhood is a model-conditioned way of proposing possible conceptual relations; a researcher must determine whether any such relation is borne out by identifiable archival traces. **Boundary:** This does not mean there are no regularities in documents before computation, nor that every computational grouping is arbitrary. ## Claim 2 — Decision trees inherit institutional categories; their visible clarity may conceal historically consequential framing

**Author claim:** Mackenzie's history of early Automatic Interaction Detector (AID) shows recursive partitioning developing within particular social-scientific and administrative concerns before its later statistical reformulation. **Evidence-supported claim:** He situates AID in Morgan and Sonquist's large social-survey analyses (p. 128), lists applications across economics, politics, media, education and family planning in **Table 6.1** (p. 129), and reproduces a 1963 income-classification tree in **Figure 6.2** (p. 130). That figure explicitly branches on then-used racial, age and educational categories and attaches annual-income values to resulting groups; these are historically located **source categories**, not categories endorsed by this note. **Warrant:** A tree can be easy to read while its discriminatory variables, labels and measurement objectives remain socially and historically contingent. The statistical neatness of a branch does not legitimate the category used for splitting. **My inference:** DDR catalogue roles, controlled terms and documentary groupings must be treated as historically produced descriptors rather than automatic indications of contribution, authorial responsibility or authority. **Boundary:** AID's income tree is not evidence of racialised or gendered practice inside DDR. More broadly, an archival catalogue is not technically a decision tree simply because it presents categories. **Consequence:** Interrogate the provenance and social assumptions of classificatory labels before using them as filters or evidence for design-historical propositions. **Practice cross-check:** Select a DDR staff/project record, trace a catalogue label to the original document and inspect how it is used in the digital interface; **TODO (specific catalogue ID, original description and test)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 128–130]

## Claim 3 — Statistical 'purity' creates differences through an explicit splitting objective

**Author claim:** A decision tree does not simply find natural divisions: it *chooses splits* to reduce a measure of class impurity and constructs a hierarchical arrangement of differences. **Evidence-supported claim:** **Figure 6.1** shows rectangular regions produced by recursive partitioning (p. 128). Mackenzie explains the Gini impurity principle as selecting cuts that increasingly separate classes (p. 134). In **Figure 6.3** (p. 135), the same iris data appear both as a readable decision tree and as plotted petal/sepal observations, while the prose explains that maximising local purity can produce overfitting (pp. 134–135). **Warrant:** 'Goodness' of a split refers to the chosen training criterion; it does not necessarily mean the resulting categories capture all relevant structure or will persist on fresh material. **My inference:** In the DDR atlas, apparent cluster neatness or a ranked separation must never serve as its own warrant for intellectual lineage, collaboration or influence. Although UMAP does *not* split points by Gini impurity, its output is likewise conditioned by representational and optimisation choices. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's demonstration is specifically supervised tree classification with labelled species. UMAP's topology and text-embedding similarity are mathematically different; claims about their stability require separate sources and tests. **Consequence:** When describing a DDR result, identify the metric or model objective actually relevant to it and examine counterexamples near apparent group boundaries. **Practice cross-check:** Compare a neat DDR visual cluster with source-level contrary or weakly supported cases, documenting the model settings; **TODO (atlas screenshot, nearest-neighbour results, PID evidence)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 128, 134–135]

## Claim 4 — Comprehensible diagrams can be unstable; stabilisation changes the classification

**Author claim:** The intuitive legibility of a decision tree is coupled to statistical fragility, so the development of tree methods has involved *limiting difference* as much as multiplying distinctions. **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie recounts 1970s objections that automated searches could make patterns out of noise and that similar candidate splits could yield different trees (pp. 129–130). He then describes **cost-complexity pruning**, in which an initially large tree is shortened against a criterion balancing fit and complexity (**Equation 6.1**, p. 136); small changes in early splits can propagate through the whole hierarchy (p. 137). He notes a subsequent shift to ensembles and random forests, where 'pattern' becomes operationally distributed rather than one readable tree (p. 137). **Warrant:** An apparently transparent visual grammar does not guarantee an interpretation's stability, and a stabilised result may depend on a selected regularisation or aggregation strategy. **My inference:** DDR's legible atlas clusters and interface connections must be evaluated under relevant changes of query, corpus, and projection settings; the historical conclusion should not rely on one attractive screenshot. **Boundary:** Tree-pruning coefficients and random-forest ensemble behaviour are *not* direct analogues of DDR's provenance guard or its UMAP parameter controls; they illustrate a distinct problem of representational robustness. **Consequence:** Preserve selected before/after interface states and document which interpretations remain defensible after a relevant computational change. **Practice cross-check:** Re-run one DDR question across two frozen, appropriately comparable configurations and review the same source records; **TODO (releases, settings, evidence cards and expert judgement)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 129–130, 136–137]

## Claim 5 — The exemplary dataset and the one-line function are already constructed pedagogical worlds

## Claim 6 — Support vector machines change the pattern by attending to difficult boundary cases

**Author claim:** The support vector machine makes differentiation depend on hard-to-separate observations near a decision boundary, rather than on fitting every observation in the interior of a class. **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie describes the movement from linear discriminant functions to support vector machines (pp. 140–141). He explains that support vectors are cases near the boundary and that margins allow overlap or misclassification (pp. 142–145). **Figure 6.5** shows the heterogeneous handwritten forms of the digit 6 (p. 143), while **Figure 6.6** diagrams a separating hyperplane and its margins around difficult cases (p. 144). **Warrant:** Cases which another procedure might treat as exceptions or nuisance variation can become constitutive of how a classifier draws the distinction. **My inference:** The most analytically productive DDR traces may likewise be *borderline*—documents that share terminology but do not support a purported relation, conflicting testimony, marginal roles, or weak catalogue associations. Deliberately testing them can reveal the instrument's interpretative limits. **Boundary:** This is a methodological **analogy**. A human-determined disputed attribution is not mathematically a support vector, and the DDR retrieval pipeline is not established here to implement an SVM. The chapter's p. 141 account places USPS digit images and MNIST close together and appears to conflate dataset resolutions; exact dimensions should be independently verified before reuse (**TODO**, not required for the historical thesis claim). **Consequence:** Include informative near misses and admissibility refusals in the evidence audit rather than evaluating only unequivocal successful retrievals. **Practice cross-check:** Use a DDR UAT case with a strong semantic match but unsupported claimed influence, record the refused claim and the direct source comparison; **TODO (test ID and source packet)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 140–145]

## Claim 7 — Dimensional expansion can produce new separations without obtaining new evidence

**Author claim:** SVM kernel methods transform the feature space so that boundaries difficult to express in the initial coordinates become separable in a newly constructed higher-dimensional representation. **Evidence-supported claim:** Mackenzie introduces non-linear mapping into high-dimensional feature space (p. 145), explains why a resulting linear hyperplane can appear curved in the original space (p. 146), and shows through Breiman's example how combinatorial feature construction greatly enlarges possible separating surfaces (**Equation 6.3**, pp. 146–147). He stresses that the kernel choices constrain which transformations are computationally workable (pp. 147–148). **Warrant:** A newly possible separation need not come from a newly observed fact: it can arise from changing the mathematical representation and the operations permitted upon it. **My inference:** A different DDR embedding version, dimensional projection or retrieval metric could make relationships look newly vivid even when the archived documents have not changed. That is an important *instrument effect* to separate from new historical evidence. **Boundary:** SVM feature-space **expansion**, kernel evaluation and UMAP's low-dimensional **projection** are different mechanisms, and none demonstrates a particular failure of DDR's bge-m3/UMAP stack. The OCR of **Equations 6.2–6.3** is not sufficiently reliable to reproduce technical symbolic statements without manually checked typesetting. **Consequence:** Attribute changing visual or retrieved relationships to the correct representational stage and preserve the source corpus/version. **Practice cross-check:** Hold a named DDR set of documents constant while recording two projection/search configurations and evaluating both against source content; **TODO (settings, PIDs and interpreted difference)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 145–148]

## Claim 8 — The history of whose technical work becomes prominent is itself unevenly visible

**Author claim:** In the discussion of SVM's canonical status, Mackenzie pauses to note the relative scarcity of prominent women's names in the machine-learning literature and specifically acknowledges Corinna Cortes's work and recognition (p. 138). **Evidence-supported claim:** He locates her within the Cortes–Vapnik paper and discusses publication prominence, historical geography and disciplinary practices (p. 138); **Table 6.2** (p. 139) documents the spread of applications and citations around SVM, although it does not furnish a demographic study of authorship. **Warrant:** A canon of algorithms can itself be uneven in how it makes contributors visible, and highly cited techniques have histories of people and institutions, not only impersonal mathematical innovations. **My inference:** This is a point of contact with the thesis's separate **feminist and situated knowledge** lens: it encourages explicit testing of named authorship, attributed technical labour and catalogue visibility in DDR, not just recovery of the usual celebrated male figures. **Boundary:** Mackenzie's observation is provisional; his chapter does not supply systematic evidence measuring women's exclusion from the entire field, and it cannot establish that a particular DDR woman's role was obscured. A citation table is not a census of contributors or proof of historical credit. **Consequence:** Treat attribution and missingness as source-specific research questions and document the status of any claim about historical labour. **Practice cross-check:** Compare selected DDR document credits, staff listings, catalogue descriptions and oral testimony in a feminist-critical use case; **TODO (named person, dates, original records, scope and verified finding)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 138–139]

## Claim 9 — Differences in *degree* need critical interrogation for differences in *kind*

**Author claim:** Mackenzie concludes that the operational dispersal of patterns risks seeing only computable gradations, but may also create opportunities to investigate qualitatively different relations *within* those gradations. **Evidence-supported claim:** On pp. 148–149 he contrasts decision trees, forests, soft margins and kernel transformations as ways of *instituting* patterns. He explicitly poses the problem of differences in degree versus differences in kind and calls for investigating the latter inside the computed former (p. 149), rather than simply declaring all machine-learning differentiation epistemically worthless. **Warrant:** A numeric difference can reveal an aspect of a case without settling what kind of historical or social relation it instantiates. Critical use requires recognising that computationally similar passages might be unlike in authority, temporal status, intentionality or documentary type. **My inference:** In DDR, two text chunks with nearby vectors may represent contemporaneous collaboration, retrospective disagreement, a catalogue repetition, or accidental shared vocabulary. To activate the archive is to make those possible *kinds* of relation visible and contestable, not force them all into a single scale of 'connection'. **Boundary:** The chapter does not supply a validated taxonomy of documentary relationships; the categories just named are researcher-side hypotheses needing archival evidence. **Consequence:** The interface and analytical writing should label evidential provenance and relationship kind independently of distance/ranking, and preserve unresolved cases. **Practice cross-check:** For one semantic neighbourhood, classify each proposed relation only after reading sources and recording contemporaneity, agency and documentary status; **TODO (PID-linked case study, date and interpretative audit)**. [@Mackenzie2017MachineLearnersArchaeology, pp. 148–149]

# Terms, definitions and thesis use

- **Pattern (Mackenzie's argument):** Not one settled operational object; what counts as pattern varies with the processes that construct recognisable differences (pp. 125–127, 149). **My use:** A model-mediated proposition for critical historical interpretation, never a self-validating finding about DDR.
- **Enunciative modality:** Mackenzie's adaptation of a Foucauldian term for how specific practices allow certain statements and distinctions to appear (pp. 126–127). **My use:** A way to ask which DDR instrument operations make a particular relationship describable. Do not mistake this for an algorithm name or a universal theory of visualisation.
- **Recursive partitioning:** Tree construction by iteratively splitting feature space according to rules and chosen criteria (**Figure 6.1**, p. 128; p. 134). **My use:** An instructive case of computational categorisation, not a description of UMAP.
- **Node impurity / purity:** A measure of class mixture used to select tree splits; Gini impurity is one example (p. 134). **My use:** A reminder that computational 'goodness' means satisfaction of a specified criterion, not independent historical validity.
- **Pruning / cost complexity:** Adjustment of a constructed tree to balance classification fit and branching complexity (pp. 136–137). **My use:** A precedent for asking about robustness and model dependence, not a method for pruning DDR archival evidence.
- **Support vector / margin:** Training cases close to a class-separating boundary and the gap through which a classifier seeks useful separation while permitting some overlap (pp. 142–145). **My use:** An analogy that motivates analysing hard DDR cases; I will never present contested attribution as an actual SVM margin.
- **Kernel mapping:** A constrained way to compare inputs in an expanded feature representation and permit separations not linear in original coordinates (pp. 145–148). **My use:** Evidence that a representation can create apparent differentiation without adding source observations.
- **Difference of degree vs difference of kind:** Mackenzie's critical distinction between calculable gradations and qualitatively different relations (p. 149). **My use:** A requirement to separate semantic proximity from the kinds of archival/historical relations it might or might not indicate.

# My critical response

The most productive feature of this chapter is its refusal to cast pattern recognition as a singular achievement. **Figure 6.1** (p. 128) shows the geometrical cuts underlying a decision tree; **Figure 6.2** (p. 130) makes the historically situated categories of an actual social-science classification uncomfortably concrete; **Figure 6.3** (p. 135) juxtaposes a clean-looking tree with measured iris cases; **Figure 6.4** (p. 140) shows multiple SVM boundary shapes; **Figure 6.5** (p. 143) presents varying handwritten sixes; and **Figure 6.6** (p. 144) shows why cases near a margin matter. Together the figures substantiate an argument more precise than the slogan that 'algorithms construct reality': *different systems manufacture different opportunities to separate, stabilise and interpret difference*. This matters for design research because a visually intelligible diagram is neither purely explanatory nor automatically a trustworthy account of the historical domain.

I would qualify the chapter in four places. **First**, Mackenzie's concept of an *enunciative modality* ranges from mathematics to scientific and social practice (pp. 126–127, 149), but these are not always demonstrated at equal empirical depth: publication prominence, technical operation and social effects need distinct evidence. **Second**, the 1963 income tree's discriminatory variables (**Figure 6.2**, p. 130) offer an especially consequential example, yet the chapter does not conduct a full historical account of the production or consequences of those categories. **Third**, the capacity to overfit in tree learning (pp. 134–137) and the soft-margin response to blurred class boundaries (pp. 142–145) are different technical dilemmas; presenting both as merely 'ambiguity' would collapse an important distinction. **Fourth**, the source's close juxtaposition of U.S. Postal Service handwriting images and MNIST on p. 141 calls for checking the image dimensions before using this chapter as an exact technical specification (**TODO**); the methodological observation about boundary cases does not require that unverified specification.

The chapter is particularly valuable as an invitation to examine **how** a difference became legible through a model and **what kind of difference** the original sources can support. It does *not* warrant treating all machine-made comparisons as fictitious. My stronger practice-led use of Mackenzie would be to document situations in which making or revising the DDR instrument exposed an unexpected contradiction, a misleading similarity or a previously underexamined trace.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** *Computational patterns become historically productive only when their method-dependent ordering of difference can be tested against the distinct kinds of relations—documentary association, recorded action, retrospective testimony and interpretation—that the archival record is capable of establishing.*

# Cross-chapter and cross-lens synthesis

Chapter 1's archaeology of heterogeneous machine-learning practice gained a diagrammatic procedure in Chapter 2, a critique of vectorised comparability in Chapter 3, a distinction between operational transformation and partial observation in Chapter 4, and an account of probabilistic error and data abundance in Chapter 5. **Chapter 6 moves the question from 'What can the machine compare, learn or optimise?' to 'What becomes a pattern or a difference under these operations?'** Its history of AID, CART and SVMs shows, in distinct ways, that a stable-looking pattern can depend on inherited categories, chosen splits, pruning rules, margins or created dimensions (pp. 128–149). This strengthens the **critical computational approaches** lens while opening a specific interface with **critical archival theory**: the digitally visible difference between two DDR traces may be an artefact of both the archive's own descriptive categories and a subsequent model's representational choices. It also points to design historiography's responsibility for distinguishing similarity from influence, and feminist/situated inquiry's responsibility for examining whose labour and difference become recognised. **My new working proposition** is that the DDR instrument can help *activate* the archive by exposing how candidate relationships are made and by allowing awkward or contested traces to remain visible rather than forcing them into a single computational grouping. **TODO (demonstrate with source-linked DDR pairs, dissenting or missingness cases, reproducible system states and independent UAT judgements).**
