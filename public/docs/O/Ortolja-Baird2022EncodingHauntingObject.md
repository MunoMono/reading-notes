# Position + moment (2–4 lines)

Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan write from history, digital humanities and cultural-heritage research through their work on the British Museum/UCL *Enlightenment Architectures* project. Their intervention draws critical archival theory, feminist data critique, museum studies and digital methods into a practical encounter with Hans Sloane's catalogues. Rather than opposing computation and critique, they ask how digital methods can be realigned to expose the silences that conventional digitisation may otherwise reproduce.

**Canon assumptions to problematise / update for 2026 (1–2 lines):**  
Machine-readability should not be confused with epistemic completeness. Contemporary AI and retrieval systems increase the stakes of their argument because archival datasets can now be combined and synthesised into fluent interpretations whose inherited absences may become even less visible to users.

# The author’s main move (1 sentence)

They try to make archival absence computationally legible by combining critical archival theory with reflective digital practice in order to prevent machine-readable heritage data from silently reproducing historical structures of attribution and exclusion.

# Three-claim evidence ledger (max 3 claims)

## Claim 1

- **Claim (plain):** Digitisation and computational modelling can reproduce and amplify absences already embedded in historical archives.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors argue that cultural scripts, power asymmetries and personal subjectivities can become embedded in apparently neutral digital tools and datasets, causing marginalised voices to be buried more deeply as information is selected and reshaped to fit models of collection. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 845–846]` Reflecting on their own TEI work, they warn that machine-readable encoding risks “the further perpetuation of historical absences” and their activation and amplification as datasets are combined in new systems. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 854]`
- **Warrant (my words):** Computational systems inherit both the surviving evidence and the historical decisions that determined what was recorded, named and made machine-readable. Encoding cannot recover information simply by formalising the record.
- **So what for my thesis (a reusable sentence):** Computational activation of the DDR archive must treat archival absence as part of the evidential structure of the dataset because machine-readability can amplify inherited patterns of visibility as readily as it can expose them.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin retrieval and Semantic Atlas: documents, people and relationships that are richly represented in the DDR corpus may acquire greater computational prominence, while poorly documented labour, students or marginalised actors may remain difficult to retrieve unless missingness itself becomes an explicit analytical concern.

## Claim 2

- **Claim (plain):** Absence can be made analytically visible without pretending that missing historical actors or information have been recovered.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** The authors survey visualisation and computational approaches designed to show missing data and cite calls to replace an “illusion of completeness” with meaningful representations of inevitable absence. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 849]` In their own work they propose “reading the catalogues for what they do not say, as much as what they do say” by analysing patterns of names, places, attribution and omission. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 855]` They explicitly acknowledge that this cannot recover individuals whose identities were never recorded, but can reveal patterns through which their absence becomes historically interpretable. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 855–857]`
- **Warrant (my words):** Missingness can therefore be treated as evidence about the structure and limits of documentation without converting absence into speculative presence.
- **So what for my thesis:** Scoped missingness can make the boundary of the DDR record analytically productive by showing where patterns of evidence, attribution or testimony stop, while refusing to infer the identities or relationships that the surviving corpus cannot establish.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin scoped-missingness queries: when a requested attribution or relation cannot be established, return the nearest documentary traces, patterns of presence/absence and provenance rather than completing the historical relation synthetically.

## Claim 3

- **Claim (plain):** Ethical digital scholarship requires the subjectivities and absences of the dataset to travel with the data rather than disappear behind the interface.
- **Evidence (quote/paraphrase + page):** In their concluding recommendations, Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan argue that contextual information explaining the subjectivities and limits of historical collections should be bundled with the resources themselves; that data absences should be communicated meaningfully; and that digital methodologies should accommodate plural voices and communities rather than reinforce a single hegemonic perspective. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 861–862]`
- **Warrant (my words):** Provenance is therefore not limited to identifying document origin. Responsible computational use requires knowledge of the archive's conditions of production, its selection mechanisms and the kinds of absence that structure the dataset.
- **So what for my thesis:** Evidential provenance in an archive-facing AI system should communicate both what a source contains and the limitations of the evidence surface from which the source has been retrieved.
- **Practice cross-check:** Turin interface: source citations expose retrieved passages and document provenance, while scoped missingness and source-type metadata communicate what the digitised DDR corpus cannot establish and prevent generated synthesis from implying completeness.

# Definitions / terms this changes (only the ones that matter)

- **Textual haunting:** the authors’ conceptualisation of individuals whose names are absent from the catalogue but whose agency and material participation remain indirectly present through the objects and relationships recorded there. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 853]`
- **Illusion of completeness:** the false impression created when digital collections expose what has been digitised without communicating what remains absent or excluded. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 849]`
- **Presence as a mirror for absence:** my shorthand for their method of examining patterns in who and what is recorded to identify structurally significant omissions without claiming to identify the missing actors themselves. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 855–857]`
- **Situated digital artefact:** a machine-readable representation produced through particular scholarly, technical and institutional decisions rather than an objective formalisation of the historical source. `[@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 855]`
- **Scoped missingness:** my methodological extension: an explicit account of what a defined archival evidence surface does not establish, grounded in observable patterns and evidential boundaries rather than speculative reconstruction.

# Related works (only if it directly connects)

- Carter (2006), *Of Things Said and Unsaid* — foundational archival account of power, silence and the exclusion of marginal groups from collective memory.

- Bernard and Balog (2025), *A Systematic Review of Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, and Ethics in Information Retrieval* — carries the problem forward into ranking, exposure and information retrieval.
