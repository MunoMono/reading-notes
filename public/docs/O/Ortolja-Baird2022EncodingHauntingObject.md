# Position + moment

Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan write from digital humanities and cultural-heritage research through the *Enlightenment Architectures* project on Hans Sloane's catalogues. Their reflexive analysis asks how machine-readable encoding may either perpetuate the archive's subjectivities or help focalise them. [@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 844–855]

# The author’s main move

They argue that digitisation and encoding are situated interpretative acts that can amplify archival silence, but computational analysis, contextual knowledge systems and plural representation can instead make those absences visible without pretending to fill them. [@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 845–862]

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Textual haunting:** indirect presence of unrecorded or unnamed agency through the relations and objects that survive. [@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 853]
- **Illusion of completeness:** false sense that a digital resource exhausts the underlying historical evidence. [@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, p. 849]
- **Situated digital artefact:** machine-readable representation produced through scholarly, technical and institutional choices. [@Ortolja-Baird2022EncodingHauntingObject, pp. 853–855]
- **Scoped missingness:** my extension: an explicit statement of what a defined evidence surface cannot establish, without speculative historical completion.

# My response

This paper is almost a methodological precedent for the DDR missingness workbench. Its central value is the distinction between making absence visible and pretending to recover what is absent. That distinction also limits generative AI: fluent synthesis can easily make a bounded digitised corpus feel complete. Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan provide the archival reason that provenance, context and absence must remain visible at the interface.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan operationalise the silence theory of Carter and Moss/Thomas in a digital environment. Bowker and Star explain why formalised categories have infrastructural consequences; Jaillant and Aske show the labour required to make archives computationally usable. Together they support a DDR method in which machine-readable evidence is explicitly partial, derived and contextual, and in which missingness is reported rather than repaired synthetically.
