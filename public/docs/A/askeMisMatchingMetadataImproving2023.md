# Position + moment

Aske and Giardinetti write from the EyCon project, which assembles conflict photography from multiple institutions and experiments with computer vision, similarity search and metadata enrichment. Their central archival problem is that digitisation has often outpaced contextual description, leaving visual records discoverable only through thin, inconsistent or historically problematic metadata. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 1–4]

# The author’s main move

The article argues that AI can improve access to large visual archives, but only if metadata is understood as historically situated and potentially biased. EyCon therefore seeks to preserve legacy descriptions, enrich them with additional context, retain links to original material supports and develop corpus-specific computational pipelines rather than replacing human description with supposedly neutral automation. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 5–17]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Digitisation does not create archival accessibility by itself because metadata determines what can be found and contextualised. **Author claim.** Aske and Giardinetti argue that large-scale digitisation has not always been matched by the creation of contextual metadata and that thin or inconsistent record data directly affects discoverability. **Evidence.** They describe institutions facing incomplete collection histories, inconsistent cataloguing practices and mass-digitisation workflows in which curatorial description is reduced to meet production targets. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 1–4] **Evidence-supported claim.** Digital availability and meaningful archival access are separate conditions. **Researcher inference.** DDR digitisation cannot be treated as a complete evidence surface simply because text or images are machine-readable. **Warrant.** Search and interpretation depend on how records have been named, contextualised and connected. **Boundary.** EyCon focuses on visual collections, where item-level semantics pose particular difficulties. **Consequence.** The thesis should analyse catalogue and metadata quality as part of archive activation, not as neutral input preparation. **Practice cross-check.** Compare DDR documents with rich catalogue description against documents whose relevance becomes visible only through source text.

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Metadata is institutionally produced and therefore varies with collecting traditions and descriptive priorities. **Author claim.** The authors contrast libraries, archives and museums, showing that each may privilege content, collection context or object provenance differently. **Evidence.** Their discussion of cross-sector visual collections shows how records of the same broad type can carry different kinds and depths of description and may become effectively invisible across institutional boundaries. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 3–5] **Evidence-supported claim.** Metadata structure is partly a product of institutional practice rather than a transparent reflection of record content. **Researcher inference.** DDR records held across RCA and V&A contexts may require cross-reading of different descriptive regimes before claims are made about prominence or absence. **Warrant.** Institutional metadata conventions affect which relations are exposed to researchers. **Boundary.** The article does not analyse the RCA or V&A specifically. **Consequence.** Repository provenance should be retained when normalising metadata for computational use. **Practice cross-check.** Preserve source repository and original field values alongside harmonised DDR corpus fields.

## Claim 3

**Claim.** “Correcting” problematic metadata can itself erase evidence of historical description and power. **Author claim.** Aske and Giardinetti argue that outdated, offensive or misleading metadata may be a barrier to access but is also a historical resource showing how records were previously interpreted. **Evidence.** Their discussion of inclusive cataloguing and “legacy data” recommends preserving original descriptions while enriching them with newer terms and additional knowledge rather than simply deleting earlier semantics. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 7–9] **Evidence-supported claim.** Descriptive change should preserve the history of description where that history has evidential value. **Researcher inference.** DDR catalogue remediation or authority reconciliation should not silently overwrite prior names, terms or associations that may reveal institutional framing. **Warrant.** Metadata can be evidence about archival practice as well as an access mechanism. **Boundary.** Preservation of harmful terminology may require careful presentation and contextual warning. **Consequence.** The instrument should distinguish original metadata from enriched or normalised metadata. **Practice cross-check.** Retain source field/value provenance when reconciling person/project names and controlled terms.

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Computational metadata is not neutral because training data inherit the priorities and exclusions of earlier digitisation. **Author claim.** The authors warn that image-recognition models are often trained on already digitised, Euro-American and contemporary visual datasets and can reproduce racial, social, cultural and economic inequities. **Evidence.** They explicitly connect digitisation priorities and historically biased training datasets to the risks of applying computer vision to archival photographs. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 6–7] **Evidence-supported claim.** Automated description can amplify pre-existing archival and data bias rather than escape it. **Researcher inference.** DDR embeddings and generated labels must be treated as dependent on the composition and descriptive history of the corpus. **Warrant.** Models learn patterns from selected representations of the archive rather than from an unmediated historical record. **Boundary.** The paper’s examples concern computer vision, not text embeddings. **Consequence.** Computational outputs should be interpreted against corpus boundaries and known descriptive asymmetries. **Practice cross-check.** Document which DDR records are excluded from embedding scope and why.

## Claim 5

**Claim.** AI-generated metadata should augment rather than replace human-produced metadata and should itself have a history. **Author claim.** Aske and Giardinetti state that machine-generated description depends on prior human annotation and argue for metadata structures capable of retaining information created across successive stages. **Evidence.** Their EyCon workflow integrates computational outputs with traditional metadata and preserves information connecting extracted images to original material supports. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 8–10] **Evidence-supported claim.** Machine enrichment is derivative and should remain traceable to both training/annotation choices and original records. **Researcher inference.** DDR generated classifications and syntheses should be stored or presented as derived representations, never as replacements for catalogue or source evidence. **Warrant.** Derivative metadata has a different epistemic status from original archival description. **Boundary.** The paper does not prescribe a general provenance standard for generated metadata. **Consequence.** The thesis should retain a layered record of source, metadata, computation and researcher synthesis. **Practice cross-check.** Keep generated evidence cards linked to original passages and catalogue identity.

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Model annotation is an interpretative act because it determines what a system is trained to notice and ignore. **Author claim.** The authors state that AI tools respond to choices made by model designers about focus, semantic associations and what to disregard. **Evidence.** In EyCon’s semi-supervised workflow, class selection and annotation precede model development, while the authors warn that harmonisation for machine learning can conflict with richer or more diverse vocabularies. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 9–10] **Evidence-supported claim.** Computational categories are designed constraints, not discovered natural classes. **Researcher inference.** DDR semantic labels, facets and query families should be documented as research decisions rather than treated as intrinsic archival structure. **Warrant.** What a model can surface is conditioned by the representational choices built into its data and interface. **Boundary.** Some computational methods, including unsupervised embeddings, do not use hand-labelled classes in the same way. **Consequence.** The thesis should expose the origin and scope of generated categories and permit ambiguous cases. **Practice cross-check.** Treat UMAP clusters and generated labels as heuristic propositions to be checked against documents.

## Claim 7

**Claim.** Cross-collection computational comparison can reveal archival contradictions that siloed catalogues conceal. **Author claim.** EyCon uses visual similarity and linked data to connect duplicate or related images held by different institutions and to compare divergent descriptions. **Evidence.** The authors give an example of the same First World War photograph carrying materially different descriptions in two French archives and argue that preserving variant records allows such contradictions to become visible. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, p. 5] **Evidence-supported claim.** Computational comparison can activate archives by making descriptive difference itself researchable. **Researcher inference.** DDR cross-document and cross-repository retrieval can be valuable when it exposes conflicting attribution or framing rather than merely merging records. **Warrant.** Contradiction across descriptions is historical information, not necessarily an error to normalise away. **Boundary.** Visual-image matching differs technically from semantic text retrieval. **Consequence.** Reconciliation should retain competing source descriptions and provenance. **Practice cross-check.** In authority reconciliation, preserve raw names/relationships where source descriptions diverge.

## Claim 8

**Claim.** Archival AI works best when tools are tailored to a bounded corpus rather than treated as universal solutions. **Author claim.** The conclusion argues for equilibrium between general tools and specific archival problems and says metadata can only ever be “good enough”; curated, corpus-specific datasets can improve relevance while supporting recovery of hidden or marginalised histories. **Evidence.** EyCon’s conclusion emphasises tailored pipelines combining layout analysis, object detection, similarity and multimodal tools, with ethical assessment of sensitive collections. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 16–17] **Evidence-supported claim.** Effective computational archival work depends on explicit corpus purpose, limits and domain-specific adaptation. **Researcher inference.** The bounded DDR instrument is methodologically stronger when it states what its defined corpus and evidence routes can and cannot support. **Warrant.** A system optimised for one archive gains interpretative value from specificity while losing claims to universality. **Boundary.** Corpus tailoring can also reinforce local biases if the corpus itself is narrow or skewed. **Consequence.** The thesis should present DDR activation as a bounded research instrument, not a general archival-AI solution. **Practice cross-check.** Use corpus-release documentation, UAT cases and scoped missingness to make the boundary operational.

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Legacy data →** inherited archival metadata whose language and assumptions may be problematic but whose history is itself evidence; retain and contextualise rather than silently erase. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 7–8]
- **Metadata enrichment →** addition of contextual or computationally derived information while retaining the source description and its provenance. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 8–10]
- **Corpus-specific archival AI →** tools and models adapted to the historical, material and descriptive characteristics of a defined collection rather than assumed to transfer neutrally across archives. [@askeMisMatchingMetadataImproving2023, pp. 16–17]

# My response

The most important contribution for DDR is the idea that metadata has a history. This prevents two opposite errors: treating catalogue description as neutral truth, or treating inherited metadata as disposable noise to be “cleaned” away before computation. The stronger approach is layered: retain the descriptive trace, enrich it transparently, and make disagreement available for research.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** Computational enrichment can activate archival description only if it preserves the history of that description and makes new machine-generated categories distinguishable from the inherited metadata they transform.

# Boundary + risk

**Boundary:** EyCon is built around photographic conflict archives and computer vision, so its technical workflow should not be directly mapped onto text-based DDR retrieval.

**Risk:** “Enrichment” can become a euphemism for overwriting or normalising away historical difference. The thesis should preserve original description and mark all derived layers explicitly.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

ISAD(G) supplies the formal descriptive logic that ties archival material to hierarchy, creator and context; Aske and Giardinetti show what happens when inherited description becomes computational data. Their account demonstrates that metadata carries prior institutional judgements, omissions and language into AI systems, while Harris’s *Archival Sliver* provides the stronger theoretical claim that such archival interventions participate in power and memory formation. For DDR, the combined proposition is that metadata is simultaneously access infrastructure, historical trace and computational input. What still requires empirical demonstration is where DDR’s own descriptive surface amplifies particular people/projects and where source-level retrieval reveals participation or concepts that catalogue metadata obscures.
