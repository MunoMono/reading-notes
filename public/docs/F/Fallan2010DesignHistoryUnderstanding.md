# Thesis job

**How this source moves the primary research question forward:** Fallan supplies a methodological vocabulary for treating design history as relational, material, networked, contested and reflexive, giving the DDR archive a historiographic lens that can move beyond heroic biography and static categories toward relations among actors, artefacts, institutions, users and ideologies.

# Position + moment

Fallan presents design history as a relatively young field still negotiating its relation to art history, cultural history, material culture, science and technology studies and broader historical method. His introduction explicitly argues for relational and reciprocal analysis of ideas and objects, ideology and practice, while later sections test methodological concepts including actor-networks, scripts, domestication, isms and paradigms. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. viii; pp. 69, 85, 93, 115, 130]

# The author’s main move

Fallan shifts the historiographic unit away from isolated designed objects and named designers toward relations, networks, material trajectories, negotiations, categories and changing cultural modes, while repeatedly warning that the analytical concepts used by historians are themselves partial constructions that require reflexive control. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. viii; pp. 11, 69, 123, 136]

# Critical-reading claims

## Claim 1

**Claim.** Design history is most useful when it studies relations among things, people and ideas rather than treating objects and designers as self-contained historical units. **Author claim.** Fallan argues that design history is becoming a history of “translations, transcriptions, transactions, transpositions and transformations” that constitute relations among things, people and ideas, and he calls for greater attention to reciprocal dynamics between idea and object, mind and matter, ideology and practice. **Evidence.** This argument is explicit in the introduction. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. viii] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source warrants a relational historiography in which designed things and design ideas acquire meaning through situated interactions and transformations. **Researcher inference.** DDR testamentary traces should therefore be mobilised through relations among documents, people, projects, teaching, institutions and later testimony rather than as isolated evidence fragments. **Warrant.** If historical meaning is produced through relations and transformations, trace-level interpretation must preserve the contexts and connections through which a proposition becomes legible. **Boundary.** Fallan’s formulation is methodological; it does not identify the specific relations that structure DDR. **Consequence.** The thesis should make relational reconstruction explicit while keeping the evidential basis of each relation inspectable. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (test named-person/project and cross-document retrieval routes against independently documented DDR relationships).

## Claim 2

**Claim.** Critical design history requires more than inserting previously excluded people into an unchanged heroic canon. **Author claim.** Fallan reviews challenges to histories of “great designs by great designers” and notes a feminist criticism of simply recovering great female designer heroines while leaving the stories and questions of conventional design history largely unchanged. **Evidence.** His historiography chapter contrasts personality-centred histories with social, feminist, Marxist, postcolonial and other approaches, then cites the problem of reproducing familiar tales of artistic heroism with female protagonists. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. 11] **Evidence-supported claim.** Inclusion without methodological change can leave the underlying historiographic structure intact. **Researcher inference.** DDR work on women’s labour and authorship should ask how attribution, project structure, cataloguing and later narration produced visibility, not merely seek additional names for the existing canon. **Warrant.** Changing the cast while retaining the same rules of historical significance does not address how exclusion is generated. **Boundary.** Fallan summarises a feminist historiographic critique rather than developing a complete feminist methodology of his own on this page. **Consequence.** Feminist reading in the thesis should be structural and evidential, examining labour, authorship and historical legibility. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (test whether DDR project records and catalogue descriptions differentially surface named leadership, acknowledged labour and unattributed participation).

## Claim 3

**Claim.** Design history has a legitimate but contested relationship to contemporary design practice. **Author claim.** Fallan stages the debate between Adrian Forty’s view that design history belongs to history and Victor Margolin’s argument for design studies that can bring historical research into relation with current practice. **Evidence.** On p. 28 Fallan presents Forty’s insistence on writing “good history” and Margolin’s defence of a productive relation between past and present. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. 28] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source supports treating the past–present relation as a historiographic question rather than an automatic justification for history. **Researcher inference.** My secondary RQ needs to preserve this tension: revisiting DDR can inform contemporary design research, but only after historical interpretation is warranted on its own terms. **Warrant.** Present usefulness cannot be allowed to dictate what the historical evidence is permitted to mean. **Boundary.** The page records a disciplinary debate rather than resolving a general theory of historical relevance. **Consequence.** The thesis should separate historical findings from the later step of evaluating contemporary implications. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (structure Chapter 7 so historical claims are established before they are opened toward contemporary design-research propositions).

## Claim 4

**Claim.** Material artefacts must be followed through their trajectories rather than reduced either to autonomous objects or to abstract symbols. **Author claim.** Fallan reviews material-culture debates and highlights the proposition that formal truth does not reveal the concrete historical circulation of things; meanings become legible through forms, uses and trajectories. **Evidence.** The discussion contrasts abstraction into social theory with approaches that “follow the things themselves” through human transactions and calculations. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. 36] **Evidence-supported claim.** Material and documentary objects gain historical meaning through circulation, use and transformation as well as through their physical or textual content. **Researcher inference.** DDR documents should be interpreted not only for what they say but also for where they sit in project sequences, institutional processes, publication routes and later archival arrangements. **Warrant.** A trace’s evidential role can change through production, reuse, description and preservation. **Boundary.** The examples discussed concern material culture broadly, not archival documents specifically. **Consequence.** Provenance and documentary trajectory should accompany semantic interpretation in the archive-activation method. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (select one DDR project family and reconstruct document sequence, reuse, catalogue description and later citation).

## Claim 5

**Claim.** Actor-network thinking is valuable to design history because it distributes agency and directs attention toward negotiations among heterogeneous actors. **Author claim.** Fallan asks who the actors are in a design process and lists management, designers, engineers, technicians, production workers, marketing, agencies, unions, interest groups, media, distribution, sales channels and users; he argues that “follow the actors” offers multiple vantage points and questions about power and negotiation. **Evidence.** The ANT discussion explicitly links network structure to meaning, attitude, position, negotiation and power. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. 69] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source supports widening historical analysis beyond privileged designers to the heterogeneous networks through which design is produced and stabilised. **Researcher inference.** DDR project history should test who becomes an actor in the surviving evidence and who is only indirectly visible through acknowledgements, administrative traces or testimony. **Warrant.** A network account changes attribution from individual authorship toward distributed participation without erasing different roles and power. **Boundary.** Fallan warns elsewhere that ANT can become an overgeneral methodological toolkit; it is a corrective, not an automatic explanatory system. **Consequence.** Actor mapping should generate questions for the archive rather than predetermine a flat network ontology. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (compare catalogue-named actors with staff lists, acknowledgements, project documents and oral-history testimony).

## Claim 6

**Claim.** Design authorship is often distributed across cooperative human and non-human systems, making single-author histories structurally inadequate. **Author claim.** Fallan uses ANT’s attention to non-human agency and cites industrial production as a complex cooperative network in which information exceeds individual memory and results cannot be attributed to one author. **Evidence.** The theory-and-methodology chapter explicitly states that design processes may be extremely cooperative and that no one person can be held responsible for a product anymore; Fallan nevertheless cautions against treating ANT as a ready-made toolkit. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. 77] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source supports distributed authorship as an analytical problem while retaining the need for methodological caution. **Researcher inference.** DDR attribution should distinguish leadership, authorship, contribution, administration, technical labour and institutional agency rather than map all participation to one undifferentiated relationship. **Warrant.** Distributed production does not imply equal agency; it requires more precise role description. **Boundary.** The industrial-production example cannot simply be transferred to every DDR project. **Consequence.** The thesis needs proposition-specific and role-specific evidence when making claims about participation. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (test role extraction and authority reconciliation against selected multi-person DDR projects).

## Claim 7

**Claim.** Designed artefacts carry both physical and socio-technical scripts, but their meanings are negotiated in use. **Author claim.** Fallan distinguishes physical scripts from socio-technical scripts and later describes domestication as a negotiated space between designers’ intentions and users’ needs in which products may be adapted, rejected or misunderstood. **Evidence.** The script discussion treats affordances and form as only part of the artefact’s action, while the domestication discussion rejects an ideal “thing in itself” and focuses on situated negotiation. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, pp. 85, 93] **Evidence-supported claim.** Intended function and actual historical meaning are not identical; use and interpretation participate in what a designed thing becomes. **Researcher inference.** DDR reports, methods and teaching materials cannot be treated as transparent evidence of reception or effect: the thesis must separate recorded intention, documented use and retrospective interpretation. **Warrant.** A design proposition may prescribe a script without determining how others understand or enact it. **Boundary.** Fallan’s concepts concern artefacts and users, so applying them to institutional documents is an analytical extension that must be labelled as such. **Consequence.** Reception should remain a distinct evidential route rather than being inferred from production records. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (identify cases where DDR intent is documented but reception evidence is absent, contradictory or retrospective).

## Claim 8

**Claim.** Historiographic categories such as “isms” and paradigms are constructed, overlapping and temporally unstable rather than neutral containers for historical evidence. **Author claim.** Fallan argues that isms are better understood as ideologies or cultural modes than as theories, then uses supplementarity and paradigm discussion to show that categories overlap, contest one another and can coexist across time rather than forming clean consecutive stages. **Evidence.** The epistemology chapter asks how isms are constructed and warns against treating them as logically bounded theories; later it argues for attention to overlapping categories and notes that design paradigms can exist simultaneously and evolve through modification. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, pp. 115, 123, 130, 136] **Evidence-supported claim.** The source supports reflexive use of analytical labels and resists clean epochal succession. **Researcher inference.** Categories such as “systematic design,” “design science,” “designerly knowing” or “cybernetic design” should be treated as historically situated analytical aids whose overlaps and contradictions remain visible. **Warrant.** If categories are themselves constructed and temporally entangled, forcing traces into mutually exclusive labels can manufacture coherence the historical record does not contain. **Boundary.** Fallan’s paradigm discussion is exploratory and analogical; it does not establish one definitive taxonomy for design history. **Consequence.** The thesis should use controlled labels for comparison while retaining ambiguous or hybrid cases and dated evidence. **Practice cross-check.** TODO (audit generated classifications and UMAP/semantic labels for forced exclusivity or false temporal succession).

# Definitions / terms this changes

- **Relational design history →** history centred on reciprocal relations among people, things, ideas, ideology and practice rather than autonomous objects or designers. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, p. viii]
- **Actor-network as corrective →** a way of asking which heterogeneous actors, negotiations and power relations constitute design; useful as a question-generating lens rather than a universal toolkit. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, pp. 69, 77]
- **Socio-technical script →** the culturally and socially mediated expectations and meanings through which artefacts invite, constrain or acquire forms of use beyond physical properties alone. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, pp. 85, 93]
- **Ism / paradigm →** analytical categories whose boundaries, coexistence and historical transformation must themselves be interrogated rather than assumed. [@Fallan2010DesignHistoryUnderstanding, pp. 115, 123, 130, 136]

# My response

Fallan gives me the strongest methodological articulation in this batch of what “critical design historiography” should actually do. The useful shift is from asking which famous designer originated an idea to asking how ideas, artefacts, institutions, users, categories and networks co-produced historical meaning. Equally important is his reflexive warning: the historian’s own categories can over-organise the past.

**Reusable thesis sentence:** A critical history of DDR must reconstruct relations and distributed agency while keeping its own analytical categories provisional, because the labels used to make contested design knowledge legible can themselves manufacture false coherence.

# Cross-source / cross-lens synthesis

Atkinson and Oppenheimer argue that design-research history must exceed canonical figures and neat generational narratives; Fallan supplies the methodological machinery for doing so by shifting attention toward relations, networks, material trajectories, negotiated meanings and reflexive categories. Boyd Davis and Gristwood then provide a DDR-adjacent case in which those concerns become empirical: Archer’s and Lansdown’s changing ideas are visible through projects, institutions, computing practices and distributed collaborations. Together these available sources make critical design historiography operational for the thesis, but the decisive step remains empirical: the DDR corpus must show which actors, relations, categories and temporal changes are actually warranted.
